A. Material properties and deformation

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definitions

  • Deformation: a change in shape (distortion), size or volume, or both, under force.
  • Material testing: measures how shape or size changes under load.

A1. Why we test materials

Materials meet forces while they are made and while they are used.

A force can move the material, turn it, or deform it.

Material testing measures how the shape or size changes under load.

Deformation means a change in shape (distortion), a change in size or volume, or both.

A2. Strength and hardness

The ability to stand a force without deforming is shown in two ways:

  • Strength: defined in different ways to suit the design.
  • Hardness: resistance to indentation or scratch.

A3. Elastic and plastic, temporary and permanent

Type Meaning Key point
Elastictemporary, recoverable after unloadingoccurs within the elastic range
Plasticpermanent, stays after unloadingoccurs after yielding
Time-dependentgrows with time under sustained stresscreep is an example

Everyday picture: a spring stretched a little returns (elastic). A paper clip bent too far stays bent (plastic).

Both kinds of deformation can depend on time or not.

A5. What a tensile test finds

A tensile test gives strength, ductility, elasticity and stiffness.

B. Tensile testing

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definition

A tensile test applies an increasing axial load to a standard specimen until it breaks; load and extension are recorded and plotted as the stress-strain curve.

2. Equipment

  • Universal testing machine (UTM): applies and measures the load.
  • Specimen: standard shape with a gauge length (at most 100 mm) in the middle; mild steel specimens follow IS 1608:2005.
  • Extensometer or strain gauge: measures the change in gauge length.

3. Equations

σ=PA0ε=L−L0L0E=σε\sigma=\dfrac{P}{A_0}\qquad\varepsilon=\dfrac{L-L_0}{L_0}\qquad E=\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}

% elongation=Lf−L0L0×100% reduction in area=A0−AfA0×100\%\ \text{elongation}=\dfrac{L_f-L_0}{L_0}\times100\qquad\%\ \text{reduction in area}=\dfrac{A_0-A_f}{A_0}\times100

P in N and A0 in mm2 give stress in N/mm2 = MPa.

4. Procedure

  1. Measure A0 and L0.
  2. Load slowly and record P and L.
  3. Calculate stress and strain at each reading.
  4. Plot stress against strain.
  5. After fracture measure final length and area.

Exam-ready conclusion: engineering stress and strain use the original area and length; Young's modulus is the slope of the linear elastic region.

B1. The idea

A tensile test applies an increasing axial load to a standard specimen until it deforms and breaks.

Load and extension are recorded.

Stress and strain are calculated from them. Plotting them gives the stress-strain curve.

B2. Equipment

  • Universal testing machine (UTM): applies and measures the tensile load.
  • Specimen: standard shape with a gauge length in the middle where elongation is measured.
  • Extensometer or strain gauge: measures the change in gauge length.

B3. Terms

  • Load: the force applied to the material during testing.
  • Strain gauge or extensometer: a device that measures change in length (strain).
  • Engineering stress: the load divided by the original cross-sectional area.
  • Engineering strain: how much the material deforms per unit length.

B4. The specimen

For mild steel the specimen is round with a uniform cross-section. It follows IS 1608:2005, identical to ISO 6892:1998.

A gauge length of at most 4 inch or 100 mm is marked in the middle part.

The elongation is studied over this length.

B5. The equations

Engineering stress uses the original area:

σ=PA0\sigma=\dfrac{P}{A_0}

P is the load in N and A0 is the original area in mm2, so the unit is N/mm2 = MPa.

Engineering strain uses the original length:

ε=L−L0L0\varepsilon=\dfrac{L-L_0}{L_0}

Young's modulus is the slope of the linear elastic region:

E=σεE=\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}

Percentage elongation:

% elongation=Lf−L0L0×100\%\ \text{elongation}=\dfrac{L_f-L_0}{L_0}\times100

Percentage reduction in area:

% reduction in area=A0−AfA0×100\%\ \text{reduction in area}=\dfrac{A_0-A_f}{A_0}\times100

L_f is the final gauge length and A_f the final minimum area.

B6. Step by step

  1. Measure the original area A0 and the gauge length L0.
  2. Load the specimen slowly and record load P and length L.
  3. For each reading, find stress P/A0 and strain (L - L0)/L0.
  4. Plot stress against strain.
  5. After fracture, measure the final length and final area, and find % elongation and % reduction in area.

C. The stress-strain curve and elastic behaviour

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Points on the curve

Point Meaning
Proportional limit up to it stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law)
Elastic limit highest stress from which the specimen recovers fully

Hooke's law: σ=E ε\sigma=E\,\varepsilon up to the proportional limit.

2. Properties in the linear elastic region

  • Stiffness: resistance to elastic deformation; measured by Young's modulus E.
  • Elastic strength: stress at the change from the elastic to the plastic range.
  • Resilience: absorbs energy elastically and recovers it.

Ur=σ22EU_r=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2E}

Modulus of resilience: strain energy per unit volume at the elastic limit; gives the capacity to withstand shock and vibration (springs).

Linear Non-linear

Exam-ready conclusion: Hooke's law holds to the proportional limit; for non-linear materials the secant and tangent moduli replace Young's modulus.

C1. The curve

The slide shows a load-extension graph with these marked: limit of proportionality, elastic limit, upper and lower yield points, tensile strength, necking at maximum load, and fracture of a cup-and-cone type.

C2. Proportional limit and Hooke's law

  • Proportional limit: the point up to which the plot is a straight line and stress is proportional to strain. Up to it the material is elastic and follows Hooke's law.
  • Hooke's law: stress is proportional to strain up to the proportional limit.

σ=E ε\sigma=E\,\varepsilon

( gives E = sigma over epsilon.)

C3. Elastic limit

The elastic limit is the point just beyond the proportional limit.

From the proportional limit to the elastic limit the material is still elastic, but stress and strain are no longer proportional.

The notes define it as the highest stress from which the specimen recovers fully on unloading.

C4. Three properties in the linear elastic region

Almost every material shows linear elastic behaviour. The properties identified in it are stiffness, elastic strength and resilience.

  • Stiffness: the ability to resist elastic deformation under load. Young's modulus E is the measure of stiffness.
  • Elastic strength: the stress at the change from the elastic range (small, recoverable strains) to the plastic range (larger, partly non-recoverable strains).
  • Resilience: the property of absorbing energy when loaded elastically and recovering it on unloading.

also says stiffness can be seen as the ability to absorb energy in the elastic range. That is what the other slides call resilience; this is logged under Flagged.

C5. Modulus of resilience

The modulus of resilience is the strain energy stored per unit volume at the elastic limit.

It gives the capacity to withstand shock and vibration. Example: springs.

Ur=σ22EU_r=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2E}

sigma is the stress at point A (the elastic limit or yield stress used) and E is Young's modulus.

Worked check with made-up round numbers (an illustration, not course data): sigma = 200 MPa and E = 200 000 MPa.

Ur=20022×200 000=0.1 MJ/m3U_r=\dfrac{200^2}{2\times200\,000}=0.1\ \text{MJ/m}^3

C6. Non-linear elastic behaviour

Sometimes stress is not proportional to strain in the elastic range. That is non-linear elastic behaviour.

Examples: rubber, polymers, grey cast iron.

Three properties apply here: secant modulus, tangent modulus and resilience.

C7. Secant and tangent modulus

There is no single Young's modulus on a curved line, so two moduli are used.

  • Secant modulus: the slope of a line from the origin to a chosen point. It is the average stiffness between the origin and that stress.
  • Tangent modulus: the slope of the tangent at a chosen point. It is the instantaneous stiffness at that stress.

C8. Resilience of a non-linear material (rubber)

The modulus of resilience does not apply, because there is no proportional limit.

The strain energy stored per unit volume is the area under the stress-strain curve. For an elastic material it is not always fully recovered.

For rubber, the loading curve and the unloading curve enclose different areas:

  • area under the loading curve: energy put in
  • area under the unloading curve: energy recovered

The recovered energy is less. The difference is lost as internal friction in the rubber.

Resilience for a non-linear elastic material is the ratio of the recoverable energy to the energy invested, as a percentage:

Resilience=area under the unloading curvearea under the loading curve×100\text{Resilience}=\dfrac{\text{area under the unloading curve}}{\text{area under the loading curve}}\times100

A low resilience is wanted for good damping. A high resilience gives low internal heat generation.

D. The plastic region of the curve

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Points after the elastic limit

Point Meaning
Yield point considerable elongation without a rise in load
Upper and lower yield points peak before the stress drop and level after it
Proof stress yield strength by the 0.2 % offset method
UTS maximum engineering stress, without necking
Fracture strength stress at fracture load: σf=Pf/A0\sigma_f=P_f/A_0
Nominal breaking stress on the original area; lower than UTS
True breaking stress on the reduced neck area; higher than UTS

2. Which yield stress

The lower yield stress is taken as the yield stress, because the upper one depends on testing speed, specimen form and cross-section shape.

3. Offset method (materials with no sharp yield)

  1. Mark 0.2 % (0.002) on the strain axis.
  2. Draw a line from it parallel to the initial straight part.
  3. The stress where the line cuts the curve is the 0.2 % offset yield strength (proof stress).

4. Why breaking stress looks lower than UTS

After the UTS the neck forms; the extensometer cannot account for it, and the stress is still worked out on the original area, so it appears lower. On the neck area it is higher than the UTS.

5. Toughness and malleability

Toughness: energy absorbed up to fracture, related to the area under the stress-strain curve. Fracture toughness is different: resistance to fracture when a crack is present.

Exam-ready conclusion: yield stress is the lower yield point or the 0.2 % offset stress; UTS is the maximum engineering stress.

D1. Yield point

Load the specimen beyond the elastic limit. It enters the elasto-plastic zone.

Here the specimen lengthens by a large amount with no visible rise in load.

Sometimes the yielding comes with a sudden drop of load and stress. Then there are two stress values:

  • upper yield point (stress): the peak before the drop
  • lower yield point (stress): the level after the drop

D2. Which yield stress to use

The lower yield stress is normally taken as the yield stress of the material.

Reason: the upper yield stress depends on the speed of testing, the form of the specimen and the shape of the cross-section.

For a material with a sharp yield point, the lower yield point is the yield strength used for design.

The notes say the same: lower yield stress is commonly used.

D3. Offset method and proof stress

Some materials show no sharp yield point. The offset method is used for them.

Steps for the 0.2 % offset yield strength:

  1. Mark a strain of 0.2 % (0.002) on the strain axis.
  2. Draw a line from that point parallel to the initial straight part of the curve.
  3. Read the stress where this line cuts the curve.
  4. That stress is the 0.2 % offset yield strength, the proof stress.

The slide shows the method on grey cast iron and the two yield points on a low-carbon steel.

D4. Ultimate tensile stress

After yield the load rises again as the material strain-hardens, and the curve goes up to its highest point, the ultimate stress.

The ultimate tensile stress (UTS), also called tensile strength, is the maximum stress the material can withstand in the plastic region without necking or failure. In a brittle material the UTS is also the breaking stress.

D5. Ductility

Percentage elongation and percentage reduction in cross-sectional area measure ductility.

% elongation=Lf−L0L0×100\%\ \text{elongation}=\dfrac{L_f-L_0}{L_0}\times100

% reduction in area=A0−AfA0×100\%\ \text{reduction in area}=\dfrac{A_0-A_f}{A_0}\times100

D6. Fracture strength

After the ultimate load the load falls. Fracture happens at a particular load.

The stress at that load is the fracture strength, also called breaking stress.

σf=PfA0\sigma_f=\dfrac{P_f}{A_0}

P_f is the fracture load and A_0 the original cross-sectional area. The unit is N/mm2.

D7. Nominal and true breaking stress

The curve of a ductile metal shows a breaking stress lower than the UTS. This is misleading.

What happens:

  1. Beyond the UTS, the cross-section shrinks near the middle of the gauge length. This is necking, or forming a waist.
  2. The extensometer is fixed at the ends of the gauge length, so it cannot account for the thinner neck.
  3. The stress is still worked out with the original area, so it appears lower. This is the nominal breaking stress.
  4. Using the reduced area at the neck gives the true breaking stress, which is considerably higher than the UTS.
  5. In a brittle material the UTS equals the breaking stress.

The notes say the same: the fracture stress on the original area is often below the UTS, and the true fracture stress, on the reduced neck area, may exceed it.

D8. Toughness and malleability

  • Toughness: the ability to absorb energy up to fracture, that is in the plastic range. It is the total area under the stress-strain curve up to fracture.
  • Fracture toughness: a property showing a material's resistance to fracture when a crack is present.
  • Malleability: the ability to undergo large plastic deformation under compressive stress.

D9. Summary of the points of the curve

Point Meaning
Proportional limit highest stress with stress proportional to strain
Elastic limit highest stress from which full recovery occurs
Yield point start of substantial plastic deformation
Proof stress yield strength by the offset method
UTS maximum engineering stress
Necking local reduction of area after the UTS
Breaking stress engineering stress at fracture on the original area
True fracture stress fracture load over the actual neck area

E. Strain hardening, Luder's bands and strain aging

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definitions

  • Strain hardening (work hardening): a ductile metal becomes harder and stronger as it is deformed plastically, at a temperature well below its melting point. Example: rolled sheet.
  • Lüders bands (Hartmann lines): bands of deformed metal at about 45 degrees to the tensile axis at the yield point.

2. Why strain hardening happens

Plastic deformation raises the dislocation density. The dislocations block each other, so motion along slip planes becomes harder; the metal is stronger.

3. Yield-point phenomenon

  1. A band of deformed metal forms at a stress raiser at the upper yield point.
  2. The load drops to the lower yield point.
  3. The band travels along the specimen: this is the yield point elongation.
  4. Several slip systems are active while it travels.

4. Strain aging (read from figure)

Property Effect
Yield stress higher
UTS higher
Total elongation smaller
Lower yield extension appears again

No mechanism is given in the course.

Exam-ready conclusion: strain hardening is strengthening by plastic deformation through dislocation interactions; Lüders bands travel at yield.

E1. Strain hardening

A ductile material becomes harder and stronger as it is deformed plastically, at a temperature well below its melting point.

It is also called work hardening.

Example from the slide: sheet metal in a rolling plant is harder and stronger after passing through the rollers to reduce thickness.

E2. The puzzle and the answer

After plastic deformation the yield stress is higher than before.

Plastic deformation raises the dislocation density. Dislocations weaken a crystal, so the deformed crystal should be weaker. That is the puzzle.

Experiments show the deformed crystal is stronger.

The reason: dislocations get in each other's way. One dislocation moving is blocked by the others, so motion along the slip planes is harder. This gives the strengthening.

E3. Yield-point phenomenon and Luder's bands

At the upper yield point a small band of deformed metal, often visible by eye, appears at stress raisers such as fillets and grooves.

When it forms, the load drops to the lower yield point.

The band then travels along the specimen. That causes the yield point elongation.

These bands lie at about 45 degrees to the tensile axis (the slide and). They are called Luder's bands, or Hartmann lines.

While the bands travel, several slip systems are active, and they give a roughly uniform dislocation distribution through the sample.

E4. Strain aging

shows a curve of a pre-strained steel with the effects of strain aging marked. Read from the figure:

  • Delta Y: change in yield stress due to strain aging. The aged curve restarts at a higher yield stress (point C above point B).
  • Delta U: change in UTS due to strain aging. The aged curve reaches a higher UTS.
  • Delta e: change in total elongation due to strain aging. The aged specimen breaks at a smaller total strain, so its elongation is smaller.
  • A strain-aged lower yield extension appears again after the pre-strain.

The slide gives no explanation of the mechanism, so none is added here. The effects are read from the figure and labelled as such.

F. Plastic deformation of crystals: slip and twinning

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definitions

  • Single crystal: one grain, no grain boundaries. Polycrystal: many crystals of different orientation with boundaries.
  • Slip: plastic deformation by movement of dislocations along preferred planes and directions.
  • Slip system: the slip plane (preferred plane) plus the slip direction (direction in that plane).
  • Twinning: the atoms on one side of the twin boundary are a mirror image of those on the other; a shear force along the boundary shifts them.

2. Rules of slip

  1. Slip occurs along certain planes and directions.
  2. The slip direction is the most closely packed direction.
  3. Slip occurs on the system with maximum shear stress, at about 45 degrees to the tensile load.

3. Twin types

Annealing twins: FCC metals. Mechanical twins: BCC and HCP metals.

4. Slip against twinning

Slip Twinning
Atom movementall atoms in a block move the same distanceplanes move a fraction of a distance
Microscopethin linesbroad lines or bands
Shear stresslowerhigher
Metalsmany slip systems, Cu (FCC)few slip systems, Zn (HCP)
Favoured byhigher temperature, gradual loadinglow temperature, impact loading
Orientationlittle changechanges in twinned region

Exam-ready conclusion: slip is the easier mode; twinning takes over where slip systems are limited.

F1. Single crystal and polycrystal

A single crystal is one grain. It has no grain boundaries.

A polycrystal is many single crystals with different orientations, with a grain boundary between neighbours.

In a single crystal, plastic deformation happens by slip and by twinning.

F2. Slip

Slip is plastic deformation caused by the movement of dislocations along preferred crystal planes and directions.

Step by step:

  1. Stress a single crystal in tension beyond its elastic limit.
  2. A step appears: the crystal divides into two blocks.
  3. One block moves relative to the other.
  4. If the load rises further, more blocks form and move.

The cause is pure shear stress across the specimen. Slip happens by the movement of dislocations.

F3. Slip plane, slip direction, slip system

Dislocations do not move equally easily on all planes and in all directions.

  • Slip plane: the preferred plane.
  • Slip direction: the direction in that plane along which the dislocation moves.
  • Slip system: the combination of slip plane and slip direction.

F4. Rules of slip

  1. Slip occurs along certain crystal planes and directions.
  2. The slip direction is the one in which the atoms are most closely packed.
  3. Of the available slip systems, slip occurs on the one with the maximum shear stress, which is at about 45 degrees to the applied tensile load.

Slip often shows as thin lines under a microscope.

F5. Twinning

Twinning is a surface imperfection. The atoms on one side of the twin boundary are a mirror image of those on the other side.

A shear force along the boundary makes the atoms shift out of position.

It occurs during deformation or heat treatment of some metals.

Type Where
Annealing twins FCC metals
Mechanical twins BCC and HCP metals

G. Fracture

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definition

Fracture is failure in which a metal separates into pieces under stress, below its melting point. It breaks atomic bonds and makes new surfaces. Three stages: crack initiation, crack propagation, failure.

2. Types

Type Name Features
Iductileextensive plastic deformation, slow crack, dull surface
IIbrittlelittle deformation, fast crack, little energy absorbed, no warning
IIIshearextensive shear and slip

3. Ductile fractures

  • Completely ductile: necks to a point, 100 % reduction in area (gold, lead).
  • Moderately ductile (cup and cone): necking, microvoids form in the neck, voids join into a crack, crack spreads, rapid final fracture. Shear lip at the rim at about 45 degrees; fibrous interior.

4. Brittle fracture

Crack moves along cleavage planes. Transgranular: through grains, grainy surface. Intergranular: along grain boundaries. Likelier at low temperature, high strain rate or loading rate.

5. Ductile-to-brittle transition

Below the transition temperature some steels fracture in a brittle way. Alloying usually raises it; use such alloys above it.

6. Griffith's criterion

A brittle material has micro-cracks that raise the stress locally. A crack propagates when the elastic strain energy released is at least the surface energy needed for the new crack surface.

Exam-ready conclusion: ductile fracture is slow with large deformation, brittle fracture is fast without warning; Griffith's criterion balances released elastic energy against surface energy.

G1. What fracture is

Fracture is a failure in which the metal separates into pieces under stress, at a temperature below the melting point.

It breaks atomic bonds and makes new surfaces.

Three stages: crack initiation, crack propagation and failure.

G2. Three types

Type Name Main points
Type Iductile fractureextensive plastic deformation, slow crack growth, dull surface
Type IIbrittle fracturelittle plastic deformation, fast crack growth, low energy absorbed
Type IIIshear fractureextensive shear deformation and slip

G3. Completely ductile fracture

The material necks down to a point. The reduction in cross-sectional area is 100 %.

It happens in soft metals such as pure gold and lead.

G4. Moderately ductile fracture: cup and cone

It is called cup-and-cone because the fracture surface has a cup on one side and a cone on the other.

Stages:

  1. The specimen necks, so the cross-section shrinks.
  2. Small cavities (microvoids) form inside the neck.
  3. They join into an elliptical crack with its long axis perpendicular to the load.
  4. The crack spreads in the direction of its long axis, and final fracture comes by rapid crack growth.

Two zones on the surface:

  • the outer rim of the neck, where shear at about 45 degrees to the load gives the fracture. It is the shear lip, with a shiny look.
  • the interior, with an irregular, fibrous look.

G5. Brittle fracture

There is no appreciable deformation and the crack moves fast.

In most brittle materials the crack moves by breaking atomic bonds along particular crystal planes, called cleavage planes.

  • Transgranular: the crack goes through the grains and gives a grainy texture.
  • Intergranular: the crack goes along the grain boundaries.

It is a catastrophic failure, with negligible plastic deformation and no prior warning. It can happen in ships, pressure vessels and bridges.

A material tends to fail by brittle fracture when the temperature drops, the strain rate rises or the loading rate rises.

G6. Shear fracture

Shear fracture is breakage caused by a force that shifts layers laterally. It results from extensive slip on the active slip plane.

G7. Ductile-to-brittle transition

Some materials, especially certain steels, get less ductile as temperature falls. Below a transition temperature the fracture is brittle, with little plastic deformation.

  • Alloying usually raises the transition temperature.
  • For an alloy the change happens over a range of temperature.
  • Such alloys should be used above the transition temperature to avoid brittle fracture.

G8. Griffith's criterion

Griffith said that a brittle material has many micro-cracks. They raise the stress locally at a nominal stress well below the theoretical value.

When a crack spreads, its sides gain surface energy. The source of that energy is the elastic strain energy released as the crack opens.

Criterion:

  • A crack propagates when the decrease in elastic energy is at least equal to the energy needed to make the new crack surface.
  • Part of the released energy goes to the new surface, and the rest becomes kinetic energy.
  • The crack propagates when a small increase of its length causes no net energy change in the system.

The criterion gives the tensile stress that just makes a critical-size crack spread as a brittle fracture.

Everyday picture: a tiny nick in a sheet of paper makes it tear easily. The nick is the micro-crack.

1. Definition

Creep is a time-dependent, slow, progressive deformation at constant stress, usually at high temperature. It becomes noticeable at about 30 % of the melting point (the slide also writes 0.5 Tm). Found in boilers, gas turbines and ovens.

H1. What creep is

Creep is a time-dependent deformation. It is a slow, progressive deformation at constant stress, usually at high temperature.

Components that meet it: boilers, gas turbines, ovens.

Creep becomes noticeable at about 30 % of the melting point. For ceramics it is 40 to 50 %. The slide also writes creep as significant at T above 0.5 Tm.

Factors: melting point, grain size, thermal stability, and also temperature and applied stress.

Everyday picture: a heavy book on a plastic shelf, left for years, slowly bends the shelf.

H2. The creep test

A constant tensile load, below the yield point, is put on a specimen kept at constant temperature.

The strain is recorded against time.

At loading there is an instantaneous strain. Only the deformation after it is creep deformation.

H3. The creep curve

H4. Primary creep

  • The creep rate decreases.
  • Deformation is rapid at first, then slower and slower as the rate approaches a fixed value.
  • The cause is work hardening of the metal.
  • It is also called transient creep, and cold creep.
  • It matters for materials that creep at room temperature.

H5. Secondary creep

  • Called steady-state creep: the creep rate is constant.
  • The rate is constant because work hardening and recovery balance each other.
  • The average rate here is the minimum creep rate.
  • It is the important part of the curve, because most working components are in this state.
  • Creep here takes place by viscous flow in the material.

H6. Tertiary creep

  • The strain rate increases.
  • It occurs when the cross-section is reduced by necking or by internal voids.
  • The stress on the smaller section rises, so strain increases faster, until fracture.

H7. Summary of the stages

Stage Creep rate Cause
Primary (transient)decreasingwork hardening raises resistance
Secondary (steady state)constant (minimum creep rate)work hardening and recovery balance
Tertiaryincreasingnecking, voids, smaller section, higher stress

H8. Effect of temperature and stress

shows how temperature or applied stress changes the creep curve. At higher temperature the creep rate increases and structural changes take place.

No numbers are given on the slide, so none are quoted here.

H9. Logarithmic creep

Creep at lower temperature is logarithmic creep:

ε=α ln⁡t\varepsilon=\alpha\,\ln t

epsilon is the strain, alpha is a constant and t is time.

At low temperature it obeys a mechanical equation of state. The strain rate at a given time depends only on the instantaneous stress and strain, not on the earlier strain history.

H10. High-temperature mechanisms

  • The vacancy concentration rises with temperature, and diffusion speeds up.
  • Recrystallization occurs because of the faster diffusion.
  • Grains in a polycrystal move relative to each other. This is grain-boundary sliding, a shear process along the boundary. It leads to intergranular cracks.
  • Atoms are more mobile and move to lower-energy positions.
  • Dislocations are also more mobile and overcome obstacles by climb.

1. Definition

Fatigue is failure of a component under cyclic loading at a stress considerably lower than the static yield stress. It is progressive, localised damage under repeated loading.

I1. What fatigue is

Fatigue is the failure of a component under cyclic loading, at a stress considerably lower than the yield stress of a static load.

It is progressive, localised structural damage. It occurs under repeated loading and unloading.

If the load is above a threshold, tiny cracks start at the surface. A crack grows to a critical size, and the structure then fractures suddenly.

Structures where it appears: planes, boats, bridges, frames, cranes, machine parts, turbines, reactor vessels, offshore platforms, transmission towers, chimneys.

Everyday picture: a paper clip bent back and forth breaks, though a single bend does not.

I2. Types of loading

Completely reversed loading. The load goes from some magnitude in one direction to the same magnitude in the opposite direction. It is periodic and symmetrical. Examples on the slide: a rotating car axle. The mean stress is zero.

Repeated loading. The load changes from zero to some value in one direction. It is periodic and asymmetrical.

The slides also show random stress fluctuations.

I3. Stress parameters

Stress range:

Δσ=σmax−σmin\Delta\sigma=\sigma_{max}-\sigma_{min}

Stress amplitude:

σa=Δσ2\sigma_a=\dfrac{\Delta\sigma}{2}

Mean stress:

σm=σmax+σmin2\sigma_m=\dfrac{\sigma_{max}+\sigma_{min}}{2}

Stress ratio:

The notes say to follow the convention given in a problem for R.

For completely reversed loading the mean stress is zero.

I4. Three steps of failure

  1. Crack initiation (nucleation): a small crack forms on the surface, often at a stress concentration. Several theories explain it, named on the slide as Orowan's theory and Cottrell and Hull's theory.
  2. Crack growth: the crack grows slowly. Each stress cycle lengthens it by a small amount.
  3. Final fracture: when the crack reaches the critical size, it spreads. The area carrying the load falls, so the stress rises, and the part finally fractures.

I5. Fatigue properties

  • Fatigue strength: the stress at which failure occurs after a specified number of cycles.
  • Fatigue limit (endurance limit): the limiting stress level below which fatigue failure does not occur. Non-ferrous materials have no endurance limit.
  • Fatigue life: the number of cycles to cause failure at a specified stress, from the S-N diagram.

I6. The S-N curve

The S-N curve plots applied stress against the number of cycles of loading on a log scale.

Some Fe and Ti alloys show a fatigue limit: the curve becomes horizontal at large N. Below that stress amplitude the material never fails, however many cycles.

Other metals have no such flat part. The notes say that non-ferrous materials have no endurance limit.

The test:

  1. A specimen of circular cross-section is held in a chuck with a weight pan hanging from the free end.
  2. A motor turns it. Each revolution bends the specimen, so the surface stress swings between equal tensile and compressive values. The mean is zero. Tension is on the top and compression on the bottom (slide figure).
  3. The test is repeated with different weights, so different stress levels.
  4. The specimen is rotated until it fails, and the number of revolutions is counted.

The results are plotted as the S-N curve.

K1. Why and what

An impact test shows how a material behaves under shock loading, which makes the specimen deform, fracture or break at once.

Most engineering parts have notches and stress raisers. So it matters how the material behaves with a notch under impact loading.

The purpose is to find the ability of the material to absorb energy in a collision. The energy is used to judge toughness, impact strength and fracture resistance.

L1. Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

SEM makes high-magnification images. A focused electron beam is scanned over the specimen and the signals made by the electron and sample interaction are detected.

L2. SEM construction

  1. Electron gun: the source of electrons. It is usually a tungsten filament, a lanthanum hexaboride (LaB6) filament or a field-emission gun.
  2. Anode: near the gun. It attracts and accelerates the electrons, typically to a few hundred eV up to tens of keV.
  3. Electromagnetic lenses: condenser and objective lenses. They focus the beam into a fine spot. Adjusting them changes the spot size and resolution.
  4. Scan coils: move the focused beam across the surface in a raster pattern, to image the whole area of interest.
  5. Sample chamber: holds the sample on a stage that can be adjusted for position, tilt and rotation.
  6. Detectors: collect the signals. The secondary electron detector (SED) gives topography. The backscattered electron detector (BSE) gives compositional contrast.

L3. SEM working

  1. The gun makes electrons, which are accelerated and focused into a narrow spot on the specimen.
  2. The beam is raster-scanned across the surface.
  3. The electrons interact with the atoms of the specimen and make signals.
  4. The detectors turn the signals into an electrical signal, which is processed into images or element maps.

L4. SEM signals

Signal Origin Information
Secondary electrons (SE)inelastic scatteringhigh-resolution surface topography and fine surface detail
Backscattered electrons (BSE)elastic scatteringcompositional contrast; distinguishes materials of different atomic number
Characteristic X-raysinner-shell ionization; the atoms emit characteristic X-rayselemental identification and local composition (EDS or EDX)

L5. SEM advantages and limits

  • Advantages: high magnification and resolution, often better than an optical microscope. It shows surface morphology and topography. The depth of field is much greater than in light microscopy, so the image looks three-dimensional.
  • Limitations: the specimen should be conductive. Non-conductive specimens can be coated. It usually needs high vacuum, which may affect some samples. Preparation and imaging can take time.

L6. X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD studies crystalline materials by measuring the angles and intensities of diffracted X-rays.

Bragg's law:

nλ=2dsin⁡θn\lambda=2d\sin\theta

  • n: order of diffraction (an integer)
  • lambda: wavelength of the X-rays
  • d: spacing between crystal planes
  • theta: angle of incidence, the Bragg angle

When X-rays hit a crystal they are scattered by the electrons. At particular angles the scattered waves reinforce each other, giving intense diffracted beams. Measuring these angles gives the interplanar spacings, which tell about the atomic structure.

L7. XRD construction

Part Role
X-ray source an X-ray tube with a metal target, usually copper, giving characteristic X-ray wavelengths
Sample holder holds the sample in a fixed position; the stage can rotate so different planes diffract
Detector at an angle to the beam; it moves around the sample and records the intensity of the diffracted X-rays
Goniometer controls precisely the angles of the beam, the sample and the detector

L8. What XRD gives, and its limits

The diffraction pattern identifies crystalline phases and gives the interplanar spacing and information about the crystal structure.

Advantages: non-destructive; high accuracy; qualitative and quantitative analysis (it can identify phases and give their relative amounts); it can estimate crystallite size and lattice strain.

Disadvantages: the sample must be a fine powder or thin, which is not always possible; it is surface sensitive, so it may not represent bulk properties; it does not work well with amorphous materials, which give no clear pattern; X-rays have limited penetration, so very thick or dense materials cannot be probed well.

Electroplating

M3. Principle and construction

  • Power supply: direct current. The positive terminal goes to the anode and the negative to the cathode.
  • Anode (positive): often made of the plating metal, for example nickel or chromium. It dissolves and releases metal ions.
  • Cathode (negative): the workpiece to be plated. The metal ions deposit on it.
  • Electrolyte: a solution of metal salts and other chemicals that lets the ions move.

The principle is electrolysis. With DC applied, the metal ions move toward the cathode. At the cathode they gain electrons (reduction) and deposit as a solid metal layer. A soluble anode replenishes the ions.

Coating thickness and quality depend on current density, temperature and plating time.

M4. Steps

  1. Clean and prepare the substrate to remove oil, oxides and contaminants.
  2. Place the workpiece and the counter electrode in an electrolyte containing coating-metal ions.
  3. Apply direct current. The ions are reduced at the cathode and form the deposit.
  4. Rinse, dry and inspect thickness, adhesion and surface quality.

M5. Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages Disadvantages
better corrosion resistance complex, hazardous waste (heavy metals, toxic chemicals)
better appearance, a bright finish uneven coating: high current density areas get thicker deposits
more hardness and wear resistance needs careful control of temperature, current density and pH
lower friction in some cases high energy consumption
good electrical conductivity thorough cleaning and preparation needed

Examples in the notes: chromium for hardness and wear resistance, nickel for corrosion resistance, zinc for corrosion protection of steel.

Chemical vapour deposition (CVD)

M6. Construction

  • Reactor chamber: a sealed chamber that withstands high temperature and keeps a controlled environment.
  • Gas delivery system: supplies the gaseous precursors, which decompose at high temperature into a solid coating.
  • Heating system: heats the chamber to the needed temperature, often 500 to 1100 deg C. Heating can be resistive, induction or microwave.
  • Substrate holder: holds the substrate so the reactant gases reach it evenly.
  • Exhaust system: removes by-products and unreacted gases.

M7. Working

The gaseous precursors enter the hot chamber and react on or near the heated substrate. They decompose and leave a thin solid coating. Temperature, pressure and the gases used decide the quality, thickness and uniformity.

Steps:

  1. Precursor delivery: reactive gases enter the chamber.
  2. Adsorption onto the hot substrate.
  3. Reaction and decomposition: a solid stays on the surface and gaseous by-products form.
  4. Desorption: the by-products leave and are removed by the exhaust.

M8. CVD advantages and disadvantages

Advantages Disadvantages
high purity, good adherence, uniform thickness high temperature limits the substrates and can distort them
many materials: metals, ceramics, diamond-like coatings expensive equipment and operation
good conformity on complex shapes toxic by-products need careful handling
high hardness, chemical stability, wear and thermal resistance limited to thin coatings; thick layers take long and may be stressed or defective

Used for hard and functional films, for example on cutting tools.

Physical vapour deposition (PVD)

M9. Construction

  • Vacuum chamber: keeps a high vacuum, which reduces contamination.
  • Target (source) material: for example titanium or chromium, in solid form. It is the source of the vapour.
  • Substrate holder: holds the part, and may rotate for an even coating.
  • Power supply: gives the energy (heat or plasma) to vaporize the target.

M10. Working

  1. Vaporization: a high-energy source such as an electric arc, an electron beam or sputtering turns the target into vapour.
  2. Transport: the vapour moves in a straight line in the vacuum toward the substrate.
  3. Deposition: the atoms condense on the cooler substrate into a thin, uniform film. With a reactive gas, compounds form, such as titanium nitride (TiN) or chromium nitride (CrN).

The notes name evaporation and sputtering as the common approaches.

M11. PVD advantages and disadvantages

Advantages Disadvantages
very hard and wear resistant expensive equipment, high-vacuum system
thin (often microns) but durable; hardly changes the dimensions needs a vacuum, which limits the size of parts
clean: no toxic by-products line-of-sight: complex shapes and hidden recesses coat poorly
wide range of materials: metals, alloys, ceramics longer process time
strong adhesion high energy consumption

adds that many PVD processes run at lower substrate temperatures than conventional CVD.

High velocity oxy-fuel (HVOF)

M12. Construction

  • Fuel and oxygen supply: separate tanks of fuel gas (kerosene, propane or hydrogen) and oxygen, mixed in controlled proportion.
  • Combustion chamber: the mixture is ignited and gives very high temperature.
  • Spray gun with nozzle: directs the combustion gases at supersonic speed and speeds up the particles toward the substrate.
  • Powder feeder: feeds metal or ceramic powder, which is heated to a molten or semi-molten state.
  • Cooling system: built-in, often water-cooled.

M13. Working

HVOF is a thermal-spray process.

Fuel and oxygen burn and make a high-speed gas stream. It drives powder particles at supersonic speed (the slide says up to 1000 m/s) toward the surface. The particles partly melt, and on impact they flatten and form a dense, hard coating. The high speed and little melting keep oxidation and porosity low, so the bond to the substrate is strong.

Steps:

  1. Clean and roughen the substrate to help bonding.
  2. Feed the coating powder into the high-velocity hot gas stream.
  3. Particles accelerate, strike and flatten.
  4. Overlapping particles build the coating. Finish and inspect if needed.

Important parameters: powder feed, fuel to oxygen ratio, spray distance, substrate temperature and surface preparation.

Used for wear-resistant metallic, carbide and cermet coatings, including WC-Co and chromium carbide systems.

M14. HVOF advantages and disadvantages

Advantages Disadvantages
high bond strength high operating cost
low porosity, so good corrosion resistance line-of-sight only
minimal oxidation skilled operators needed
many materials: metals, alloys, ceramics limits on thick layers
superior wear and corrosion resistance
