1.2 Elastic energy

Modulus of resilience (linear elastic):

Ur=σ22EU_r=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2E}

sigma is the stress at the elastic limit or yield stress used, E is Young's modulus.

Non-linear elastic materials:

  • secant modulus: slope of the line from the origin to a chosen point, average stiffness
  • tangent modulus: slope of the tangent at a chosen point, instantaneous stiffness
  • strain energy per unit volume: the area under the stress-strain curve
  • resilience for a non-linear material:

Resilience=area under the unloading curvearea under the loading curve×100\text{Resilience}=\dfrac{\text{area under the unloading curve}}{\text{area under the loading curve}}\times100

1.3 Fatigue stress parameters

Δσ=σmax−σminσa=Δσ2σm=σmax+σmin2R=σminσmax\Delta\sigma=\sigma_{max}-\sigma_{min}\qquad\sigma_a=\dfrac{\Delta\sigma}{2}\qquad\sigma_m=\dfrac{\sigma_{max}+\sigma_{min}}{2}\qquad R=\dfrac{\sigma_{min}}{\sigma_{max}}

For completely reversed loading the mean stress is zero. Follow the convention given in the problem for R.

1.4 Creep

Logarithmic creep (low temperature):

ε=αln⁡t\varepsilon=\alpha\ln t

epsilon strain, alpha a constant, t time.

1.5 Hardness

Brinell hardness number:

BHN=PπD2[D−D2−d2]BHN=\dfrac{P}{\dfrac{\pi D}{2}\left[D-\sqrt{D^2-d^2}\right]}

P load in kgf, D ball diameter in mm, d mean impression diameter in mm.

Vickers hardness:

VHN=1.854 Pd2VHN=\dfrac{1.854\,P}{d^2}

P in kgf, d mean diagonal in mm.

Rockwell: no formula; read the scale.

1.6 X-ray diffraction

Bragg's law:

nλ=2dsin⁡θn\lambda=2d\sin\theta

n order of diffraction, lambda wavelength, d interplanar spacing, theta Bragg angle.

1.12 Fatigue

  • Three steps: crack initiation, crack growth, final fracture.
  • Fatigue strength: stress at failure for a specified number of cycles.
  • Fatigue limit: stress below which failure does not occur (some Fe and Ti alloys); non-ferrous metals have none.
  • Fatigue life: cycles to failure at a specified stress.
  • Low cycle: N below 10510^5, high loads, plastic and elastic. High cycle: N above 10510^5, low loads, elastic.
  • Factors: stress concentrations, corrosion, residual stress, surface finish, high temperature.

2. Methods with one solved example each

The course question bank, Q and A and assignment give no numbers for these quantities. Every example below uses made-up round numbers, labelled as an illustration, and each was checked by computing it. Formulas come from the sheet above.

Method 1. Stress, strain and Young's modulus

  1. Measure the original area A0 and original gauge length L0.
  2. Read the load P and the loaded length L.
  3. Stress is P divided by A0.
  4. Strain is (L - L0) divided by L0.
  5. In the linear region E is stress divided by strain.

Illustration: P = 20 000 N, A0 = 100 mm2, L0 = 50 mm, L = 50.1 mm (in the linear region).

σ=20 000100=200 MPa\sigma=\dfrac{20\,000}{100}=200\ \text{MPa}

ε=50.1−5050=0.002\varepsilon=\dfrac{50.1-50}{50}=0.002

E=2000.002=100 000 MPaE=\dfrac{200}{0.002}=100\,000\ \text{MPa}

Answer: stress 200 MPa, strain 0.002, E = 100 000 MPa (100 GPa).

Method 2. Percentage elongation and reduction in area

  1. Measure the final gauge length L_f and the final minimum area A_f after fracture.
  2. Use the two ductility formulas.

Illustration: L0 = 50 mm, L_f = 62 mm, A0 = 100 mm2, A_f = 64 mm2.

% elongation=62−5050×100=24 %\%\ \text{elongation}=\dfrac{62-50}{50}\times100=24\ \%

% reduction in area=100−64100×100=36 %\%\ \text{reduction in area}=\dfrac{100-64}{100}\times100=36\ \%

Answer: 24 % elongation and 36 % reduction in area.

Method 3. Fracture strength, nominal and true

  1. Fracture strength is the fracture load over the original area.
  2. True fracture stress uses the actual neck area.

Illustration: P_f = 18 000 N, A0 = 100 mm2, neck area 40 mm2.

σf=18 000100=180 MPa(nominal)\sigma_f=\dfrac{18\,000}{100}=180\ \text{MPa}\quad(\text{nominal})

σtrue=18 00040=450 MPa\sigma_{true}=\dfrac{18\,000}{40}=450\ \text{MPa}

Answer: nominal 180 MPa, true 450 MPa. The true value is higher because the neck area is smaller.

Method 4. Modulus of resilience

  1. Take the stress at the elastic limit or yield stress, sigma.
  2. Take Young's modulus E in the same units.
  3. Use the formula.

Illustration: sigma = 250 MPa, E = 200 000 MPa.

Ur=σ22E=25022×200 000U_r=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2E}=\dfrac{250^2}{2\times200\,000}

Ur=0.156 MJ/m3U_r=0.156\ \text{MJ/m}^3

Answer: 0.156 MJ/m3 (strain energy per unit volume at the elastic limit).

Method 5. Secant and tangent modulus; resilience of a non-linear material

  1. Secant modulus: pick a point on the curve and divide its stress by its strain (slope from the origin).
  2. Tangent modulus: draw the tangent at the point and take its slope.
  3. Resilience of a non-linear elastic material: area under the unloading curve over area under the loading curve, times 100.

Illustration: a point at stress 10 MPa and strain 0.02; a tangent through it rises 6 MPa over 0.03 strain; areas 40 (loading) and 30 (unloading) in the same units.

Esecant=100.02=500 MPaE_{secant}=\dfrac{10}{0.02}=500\ \text{MPa}

Etangent=60.03=200 MPaE_{tangent}=\dfrac{6}{0.03}=200\ \text{MPa}

Resilience=3040×100=75 %\text{Resilience}=\dfrac{30}{40}\times100=75\ \%

Answer: secant 500 MPa, tangent 200 MPa, resilience 75 %, so 25 % of the energy is lost to internal friction.

Method 6. The 0.2 % offset yield strength (graphical)

  1. Mark 0.002 on the strain axis.
  2. Draw a line from that point parallel to the straight part of the curve.
  3. Read the stress where the line meets the curve.

The result depends on the plotted curve. The course gives no coordinate values, so no number is derived here. See the figure in Concepts D3.

Method 7. Fatigue stress parameters

  1. Read sigma_max and sigma_min (tension positive).
  2. Range is sigma_max minus sigma_min.
  3. Amplitude is half the range.
  4. Mean is the average of the two.
  5. R is sigma_min over sigma_max.

Illustration: sigma_max = 300 MPa, sigma_min = -100 MPa.

Δσ=300−(−100)=400 MPa\Delta\sigma=300-(-100)=400\ \text{MPa}

σa=4002=200 MPa\sigma_a=\dfrac{400}{2}=200\ \text{MPa}

σm=300+(−100)2=100 MPa\sigma_m=\dfrac{300+(-100)}{2}=100\ \text{MPa}

R=−100300=−0.333R=\dfrac{-100}{300}=-0.333

Answer: range 400, amplitude 200, mean 100 MPa, R = -0.333. For completely reversed loading, sigma_min = -sigma_max, so the mean is 0 and R = -1.

Method 8. Hardness numbers

Brinell. Use P, D, d in kgf and mm.

Illustration: P = 3000 kgf, D = 10 mm, d = 4 mm.

BHN=3000π×102[10−102−42]BHN=\dfrac{3000}{\dfrac{\pi\times10}{2}\left[10-\sqrt{10^2-4^2}\right]}

BHN=300015.708×0.8348≈228.8BHN=\dfrac{3000}{15.708\times0.8348}\approx228.8

Vickers. Use P in kgf and the mean diagonal d in mm.

Illustration: P = 30 kgf, d = 0.5 mm.

VHN=1.854×300.52=222.5VHN=\dfrac{1.854\times30}{0.5^2}=222.5

Rockwell: no calculation; read the scale (B for a ball on softer steel, C for the diamond cone on harder material).

Method 9. Bragg's law

  1. Take n (use 1 for the first-order reflection), the wavelength lambda and the spacing d or the angle.
  2. Use n lambda = 2 d sin(theta).

Illustration: n = 1, lambda = 0.154 nm, d = 0.2 nm.

sin⁡θ=nλ2d=0.1542×0.2=0.385\sin\theta=\dfrac{n\lambda}{2d}=\dfrac{0.154}{2\times0.2}=0.385

θ=22.6∘\theta=22.6^\circ

Answer: theta = 22.6 degrees. Going the other way, measured angles give d.

Method 10. Logarithmic creep

  1. Take the constant alpha.
  2. Strain is alpha times ln t.

Illustration: alpha = 0.002, t = 10 and then t = 100 (same time unit).

ε(10)=0.002ln⁡10=0.0046\varepsilon(10)=0.002\ln10=0.0046

ε(100)=0.002ln⁡100=0.0092\varepsilon(100)=0.002\ln100=0.0092

Answer: a ten-fold increase in time doubles the strain from 0.0046 to 0.0092 here. This law is for low-temperature creep.
