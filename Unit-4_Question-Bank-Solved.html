Group Part 1 Easy: Q1 to Q10

U4-Q1Q1 | Easy | Strength

Define what strength means as a material property prior to failure.

Solution

Strength is the greatest stress that a material can withstand before failure. It can be given as yield strength, tensile strength or fracture strength.

Also Q&A answer 1 (the Q&A item is the same question). See Concepts A4.

U4-Q2Q2 | Easy | Ductility

State the definition of ductility in terms of plastic deformation before failure.

Solution

Ductility is the property that lets a material undergo considerable plastic deformation under a load before failure. It is measured by percentage elongation and percentage reduction in area.

U4-Q3Q3 | Easy | Elasticity

Explain the property of elasticity that allows a material to retain original dimensions.

Solution

Elasticity is the property that allows a material to retain its original dimensions after the deforming load is removed. The deformation is temporary and recoverable, and it occurs within the elastic range.

Example: a spring stretched a little returns to its length.

U4-Q4Q4 | Easy | Stiffness

Describe stiffness as the material property that withstands high stress without great strain.

Solution

Stiffness is the property that lets a material withstand high stress without great strain. It is the resistance to elastic deformation. Young's modulus is its measure in the linear elastic region.

U4-Q8Q8 | Easy | Engineering stress

State the formula and definition for engineering stress in a tensile test.

Solution

Engineering stress is the applied load divided by the original cross-sectional area:

σ=PA0\sigma=\dfrac{P}{A_0}

P is the load in N and A0 the original area in mm2. The unit is N/mm2 = MPa. See Method 1.

U4-Q9Q9 | Easy | Engineering strain

Define engineering strain as the amount a material deforms per unit length.

Solution

Engineering strain is how much the material deforms per unit original length. It uses the original gauge length:

ε=L−L0L0\varepsilon=\dfrac{L-L_0}{L_0}

L0 is the original length and L the loaded length.

U4-Q12Q12 | Easy | Proportional limit

Define the proportional limit on a stress-strain curve.

Solution

The proportional limit is the point up to which the stress-strain plot is a straight line and stress is proportional to strain. Up to it the material is elastic and follows Hooke's law strictly. The notes call it the highest stress for which stress is directly proportional to strain.

U4-Q13Q13 | Easy | Hooke's law

Explain what Hooke's Law states regarding stress and strain up to the proportional limit.

Solution

Up to the proportional limit, stress is directly proportional to strain, and the material is elastic:

σ=E ε\sigma=E\,\varepsilon

The constant of proportionality is Young's modulus E, the slope of the straight line.

This is also assignment item 1. See Concepts C2.

U4-Q14Q14 | Easy | Elastic limit

Describe the elastic limit as the point just beyond the proportional limit.

Solution

The elastic limit is the point just beyond the proportional limit. From the proportional limit to the elastic limit the material is still elastic, but stress and strain are not proportional. The notes define it as the highest stress from which the specimen recovers fully on unloading.

U4-Q16Q16 | Easy | Stiffness

Define stiffness as the ability of a material to resist elastic deformation under load.

Solution

Stiffness is the ability of a material to resist elastic deformation under load. Young's modulus E is the measure of stiffness.

U4-Q17Q17 | Easy | Young's modulus

State how Young's modulus or modulus of elasticity acts as a measure of material stiffness.

Solution

The modulus of elasticity, or Young's modulus E, is itself the measure of stiffness. It is the slope of the linear elastic part of the stress-strain curve:

E=σεE=\dfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}

A steeper line means a larger E: the material shows less strain for the same stress, so it is stiffer.

U4-Q18Q18 | Easy | Elastic strength

Define elastic strength as the stress representing the transition from elastic to plastic range.

Solution

Elastic strength is the stress that represents the transition from the elastic range, where strains are small and recoverable, to the plastic range, where strains are larger and partly non-recoverable.

U4-Q19Q19 | Easy | Resilience

State the definition of resilience and its associated modulus property.

Solution

Resilience is the property of a material to absorb energy when it is elastically loaded and to recover the energy on removal of the load. The associated property is the modulus of resilience.

It gives the capacity to withstand shock and vibration, for example in springs.

U4-Q20Q20 | Easy | Modulus of resilience

Define the modulus of resilience as the strain energy stored per unit volume at the elastic limit.

Solution

The modulus of resilience is the strain energy stored per unit volume at the elastic limit.

For a linear elastic material:

Ur=σ22EU_r=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2E}

sigma is the stress at the elastic limit and E is Young's modulus. See Method 4.

U4-Q24Q24 | Easy | Secant modulus

Define the secant modulus for non-linear elastic materials.

Solution

The elastic part of the curve is non-linear, so Young's modulus cannot be found. The secant modulus is used instead. It is the slope of a secant drawn from the origin to a given point on the stress-strain curve.

It is the average stiffness between the origin and that stress.

U4-Q25Q25 | Easy | Tangent modulus

Describe the tangent modulus as the slope of the tangent drawn at any point on a non-linear curve.

Solution

The tangent modulus is the slope of the tangent drawn at any point on the non-linear stress-strain curve. It gives the instantaneous stiffness of the material at that stress.

U4-Q26Q26 | Easy | Resilience of a non-linear material

State how resilience is defined for non-linear elastic materials using loading and unloading curves.

Solution

The modulus of resilience does not apply, because there is no proportional limit. Instead, resilience is the ratio of the recoverable energy to the energy put in during deformation, as a percentage:

Resilience=area under the unloading curvearea under the loading curve×100\text{Resilience}=\dfrac{\text{area under the unloading curve}}{\text{area under the loading curve}}\times100

The area under the loading curve is the energy put in. The area under the unloading curve is the energy recovered. The difference is lost as internal friction. See Method 5.

U4-Q27Q27 | Easy | Yield point

Define the yield point where elongation occurs considerably without an increase in load.

Solution

When the specimen is loaded beyond the elastic limit it enters the elasto-plastic zone. The specimen lengthens by a considerable amount without a perceptible rise in load. This is the yield point.

Sometimes yielding comes with a sudden drop of load and stress. The two stress values are the upper yield point and the lower yield point.

U4-Q28Q28 | Easy | Lower yield stress

Explain why the lower yield stress is normally considered the yield stress of a material.

Solution

The upper yield stress is affected by the speed of testing, the form of the specimen and the shape of the cross-section. So the lower yield stress is normally taken as the yield stress of the material.

For design, the lower yield point is used as the yield strength for a material with a sharp yield point.

U4-Q29Q29 | Easy | Offset method

Describe the offset method used for materials that do not exhibit a sharp yield point.

Solution

For a material with no sharp yield point, the offset method is used. For the 0.2 % offset:

  1. Mark a strain of 0.2 % (0.002) on the strain axis.
  2. Draw a line from that point parallel to the initial straight part of the curve.
  3. The stress where the line meets the curve is the 0.2 % offset yield strength, also called the proof stress.

See the figure in Concepts D3.

U4-Q30Q30 | Easy | Ultimate tensile stress

Define ultimate tensile stress (UTS) as the maximum stress withstandable in the plastic region without necking.

Solution

The ultimate tensile stress (UTS), also called the tensile strength, is the maximum stress the material can withstand in the plastic region without necking or failure. It is the highest engineering stress on the curve. In a brittle material it is also the breaking stress.

U4-Q32Q32 | Easy | Fracture strength

Define fracture strength as the stress corresponding to the load where actual fracture takes place.

Solution

After the ultimate load, the load drops, and at a particular load the specimen fractures. The stress at that load is the fracture strength, or breaking stress, of the material.

U4-Q34Q34 | Easy | Toughness

Define toughness as the ability of a material to absorb energy up to fracture or in the plastic range.

Solution

Toughness is the ability of a material to absorb energy up to fracture, that is, in the plastic range. It is related to the total area under the stress-strain curve up to fracture.

Fracture toughness is a different property: the resistance to fracture when a crack is present.

U4-Q36Q36 | Easy | Strain hardening

Solution

Strain hardening, also called work hardening, is the phenomenon in which a ductile material becomes harder and stronger as it is deformed plastically, at a temperature well below its melting point.

Example: sheet metal passed through rollers to reduce thickness becomes harder and stronger. The cause is the interaction of dislocations, which blocks further dislocation motion. See Concepts E.

U4-Q40Q40 | Easy | Slip system

Define a slip system as the combination of a preferred slip plane and a specific slip direction.

Solution

Dislocations move more easily on some planes and in some directions than on others. The preferred plane is the slip plane. The direction along which the dislocation moves in that plane is the slip direction. The combination of the two is a slip system.

U4-Q41Q41 | Easy | Rules of slip

State the rules governing slip regarding closely packed atomic directions and maximum shear stress.

Solution

Slip is governed by these rules:

  1. Slip occurs along certain crystallographic planes and directions.
  2. The slip direction is the direction in which the atoms are most closely packed.
  3. Of the available slip systems, slip occurs on the one where the shear stress is maximum, that is at about 45 degrees to the applied tensile load.
U4-Q42Q42 | Easy | Twinning

Define twinning as a surface imperfection where the atomic arrangement on one side is a mirror reflection.

Solution

Twinning is a surface imperfection in which the atomic arrangement on one side of the twin boundary is a mirror reflection of the arrangement on the other side. A shear force acting along the twin boundary makes the atoms shift out of position. Twinning occurs during deformation or heat treatment of some metals.

U4-Q43Q43 | Easy | Annealing and mechanical twins

U4-Q47Q47 | Easy | Completely ductile fracture

Describe completely ductile fracture as seen in soft metals like pure gold and lead.

Solution

In completely ductile fracture the material necks down to a point and fractures, showing a 100 % reduction in cross-sectional area. It takes place in soft metals such as pure gold (Au) and lead (Pb). It is one of the two kinds of ductile fracture: slow crack growth after extensive plastic deformation, with a dull fracture surface.

U4-Q48Q48 | Easy | Cup and cone

Explain why moderately ductile fracture is commonly referred to as a cup and cone fracture.

Solution

The fracture surface has a cup-like part on one piece and a cone-like part on the other. That is why moderately ductile fracture is called cup and cone fracture.

Stages: necking, microvoids form, the voids join into a crack, and the crack spreads to final fracture. The outer rim is a shear lip at about 45 degrees to the load.

U4-Q49Q49 | Easy | Brittle fracture

Define brittle fracture and state how crack propagation occurs along specific cleavage planes.

Solution

Brittle fracture takes place with no appreciable deformation and with fast crack propagation, little energy absorbed, and no prior warning.

In most brittle materials the crack moves by breaking atomic bonds along specific crystallographic planes called cleavage planes. If the crack goes through the grains it is transgranular, giving a grainy texture. If it goes along grain boundaries it is intergranular.

U4-Q50Q50 | Easy | Ductile-to-brittle transition

State the condition of ductile-to-brittle transition when material temperature drops below a transition threshold.

Solution

The ductile-to-brittle transition occurs when the temperature falls below a transition temperature. Below it the material fractures in a brittle way, with little plastic deformation. Alloying usually raises the transition temperature, and an alloy changes over a range of temperature. Such alloys should be used above the transition temperature.

U4-Q53Q53 | Easy | Logarithmic creep

Define logarithmic creep occurring at lower temperatures and state its governing equation.

Solution

Creep that occurs at lower temperature is called logarithmic creep. Its equation is

epsilon is the strain, alpha is a constant and t is time. At low temperature the strain rate at a given time depends only on the instantaneous stress and strain, not on the previous strain history. See Method 10.

Define fatigue as the failure of a component subjected to cyclic loading at a stress considerably lower than static yield stress.

Solution

Fatigue is the failure of a component subjected to cyclic loading at a stress considerably lower than the yield stress for a static load. It is progressive, localized structural damage that occurs under repeated loading and unloading.

U4-Q55Q55 | Easy | Steps of fatigue failure

List the three chronological steps involved in fatigue failure.

Solution
  1. Crack initiation (nucleation): a small crack forms on the surface.
  2. Crack growth: the crack grows slowly, each stress cycle lengthening it a little.
  3. Final fracture: at the critical size the crack propagates and the part fractures.

U4-Q61Q61 | Medium | Permanent and temporary deformation

Analyze how permanent deformation differs from temporary deformation in terms of time dependency and load removal.

Solution

Definition. Temporary (elastic) deformation is recoverable after unloading. Permanent (plastic) deformation stays after unloading.

U4-Q62Q62 | Medium | Extensometer and strain gage

Explain how an extensometer or strain gage functions to capture real-time elongation during a tensile test.

Solution

Purpose. Both measure the change in the gauge length of the specimen, which gives the strain at each load. Stress comes from the load cell, strain from this device, and together they give the stress-strain curve.

Strain gage Extensometer
Fittedbonded to the specimen surface inside the gauge lengthclip-on device whose knife-edges clamp on the parallel gauge length
Workingthe foil grid stretches with the specimen and its electrical resistance changesthe knife-edges move apart as the specimen deforms; internal linkages or transducers (such as an LVDT) turn the movement into a signal
Readingresistance change measured in a Wheatstone bridge and converted to a signal for micro-straincontinuous electronic signal of the elongation

(Working details are from the course Q and A, Q&A 4.)

Steps in the test.

  1. Fit the device on the gauge length.
  2. Load the specimen steadily.
  3. The device sends the elongation as an electrical signal while the load is read.
  4. The machine plots load against elongation, and from the original area and length the stress-strain curve is built, up to fracture.

Note. The extensometer cannot account for the neck, so the stress it gives is not corrected for the reduced area; this is why the nominal breaking stress is lower than the true one (see Q44).

Same as Q&A 4.

U4-Q63Q63 | Medium | Linear portion and Hooke's law

Examine why the initial linear portion of a stress-strain curve adheres strictly to Hooke's Law.

Solution

What the course says. The initial straight part runs up to the proportional limit. Up to it the plot is a straight line, stress is proportional to strain, the material is elastic, and it follows Hooke's law:

σ=E ε\sigma=E\,\varepsilon

The constant of proportionality E, the slope of the line, is Young's modulus, the measure of stiffness.

Explanation from the course.

  1. Stress and strain increase in the same ratio, so the plot is straight and its slope is constant.
  2. The deformation is elastic, so on unloading the material recovers fully (temporary deformation, see Q61).
  3. The line ends at the proportional limit. Beyond it the line is no longer straight, so Hooke's law no longer holds. Between the proportional limit and the elastic limit the material is still elastic but stress and strain are not proportional.

Table: the three regions.

Region Straight? Elastic? Hooke's law
Up to proportional limityesyesholds
Proportional to elastic limitnoyesdoes not hold
Beyond elastic limitnono (plastic)does not hold

Flagged / limit of the course material. The slides and notes state that the line is straight and obeys Hooke's law up to the proportional limit, but they give no further physical reason for why. So no reason beyond these statements is given here. See Concepts C2.

U4-Q65Q65 | Medium | Secant modulus

Calculate or explain how secant modulus provides a measure of average stiffness for non-linear elastic materials.

Solution

Definition. For a non-linear elastic material the elastic part of the curve is curved, so Young's modulus cannot be found. The secant modulus is used instead: the slope of the secant drawn from the origin to a given point on the stress-strain curve.

Formula. For the chosen point with stress and strain:

Esecant=σ1ε1E_{\text{secant}}=\dfrac{\sigma_1}{\varepsilon_1}

sigma_1 and epsilon_1 are the stress and strain at the chosen point. It is the slope of the line from the origin to that point.

Why it is the average stiffness. The line goes from the origin to the point, so its slope is the stress per unit strain averaged over the whole range from zero to that stress. It changes with the point chosen, so the stress at which it is quoted must be stated.

Compare with the tangent modulus. The tangent modulus is the slope of the tangent at the point and gives the instantaneous stiffness.

Secant modulus Tangent modulus
Line drawnfrom the origin to the pointtouching the curve at the point
Givesaverage stiffness up to that stressinstantaneous stiffness at that stress

Numerical illustration (made-up round numbers, not course data). If the chosen point has stress 10 MPa at strain 0.05:

Esecant=100.05=200 MPaE_{\text{secant}}=\dfrac{10}{0.05}=200\ \text{MPa}

U4-Q66Q66 | Medium | Resilience of rubber

Analyze why the traditional modulus of resilience is inapplicable to non-linear elastic materials like rubber.

Solution

Traditional modulus of resilience. It is the strain energy per unit volume at the elastic limit:

Ur=σ22EU_r=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2E}

It needs a single Young's modulus E and a proportional limit, because the energy is the triangular area under a straight line.

Why it fails for rubber.

U4-Q76Q76 | Medium | Griffith criterion

Explain Griffith's criteria of brittle fracture regarding elastic strain energy release and micro-crack surface energy creation.

Solution

Principle. A brittle material has many micro-cracks. They raise the stress locally, so fracture happens at a nominal stress well below the theoretical value.

Energy balance.

  1. When a crack spreads, its sides gain surface energy. Creating new crack surface needs energy.
  2. The source of that energy is the elastic strain energy released as the crack opens.
  3. A crack propagates when the decrease in elastic energy is at least equal to the energy needed to make the new surface.
  4. Part of the released energy goes into the new surface and the rest becomes kinetic energy.
  5. The crack propagates when a small increase of its length causes no net energy change in the system.
Energy Effect
Elastic strain energy released decreases as the crack grows; supplies energy
Surface energy of new crack faces increases as the crack grows; takes energy

The criterion gives the tensile stress that just makes a crack of critical size spread as a brittle fracture.

U4-Q102Q102 | Tough | Creep: climb, diffusion and grain boundary sliding

Analyze the interplay between dislocation climb, atomic diffusion, and grain boundary sliding during the transition from secondary steady-state creep to tertiary void-induced rupture.

Solution

Secondary stage. The creep rate is constant because work hardening and recovery balance each other. Recovery relies on mobile atoms and dislocations: diffusion speeds up as vacancy concentration rises with temperature, and dislocations overcome obstacles by climb.

Grain-boundary sliding. Grains also slide relative to each other by shear along the boundary, which leads to intergranular cracks.

Transition to tertiary. The cross-section is reduced by necking or by internal voids. The stress on the smaller section rises, so the strain rate rises until fracture.

Mechanism Role Effect on the creep curve
Atomic diffusion (more vacancies)makes climb and recovery possible; makes recrystallization occurkeeps the secondary rate steady while balanced
Dislocation climblets dislocations pass obstaclesrecovery term in the balance
Grain-boundary slidingboundary shear; intergranular cracksadds damage; cracks and voids reduce the area
Voids and neckingsmaller load-bearing sectionhigher stress, rate rises (tertiary)

Interplay.

  1. In the secondary stage hardening and recovery (diffusion and climb) cancel, giving a constant rate.
  2. Grain-boundary sliding and the damage it causes accumulate with time.
  3. When the damage reduces the section enough, the true stress rises and the balance is lost.
  4. The rate increases and the specimen ruptures.

Analysis of the transition.

U4-Q120Q120 | Tough | Cup and cone fracture and toughness

Solution

Microstructural evolution.

Step What happens
1 the specimen necks and the cross-section shrinks
2 microvoids form inside the neck
3 the voids join (coalesce) into an elliptical crack with its long axis perpendicular to the load
4 the crack spreads along its long axis; final fracture by rapid crack growth

Link to toughness.

Property Course statement
Toughness the ability to absorb energy up to fracture, in the plastic range; related to the total area under the stress-strain curve
Fracture toughness a different property: resistance to fracture when a crack is present
Ductile fracture slow crack growth after extensive plastic deformation, so much energy is absorbed; brittle fracture absorbs little energy

Critical analysis. A cup and cone fracture comes after considerable plastic deformation and a slow, void-by-void crack, with a final shear at the rim. This means the metal has absorbed a large amount of energy before it separated, which is the meaning of toughness in the course. Brittle fracture is the opposite: little deformation, fast crack, little energy.
