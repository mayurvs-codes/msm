U3-Q1Q1 | Easy | Solid solution

Define a solid solution and explain the basic concept of solute and solvent atoms with suitable examples.

Solution

Definition. A solid solution is an alloy of two or more elements mixed uniformly in the solid state, so that it forms a single phase.

Solvent and solute.

  • Solvent: the base metal, whose lattice is the host.
  • Solute: the alloying element dissolved in the host lattice.

Examples. Copper dissolved in nickel (Monel), and brass. Everyday picture: sugar (solute) in tea (solvent) gives one liquid.

U3-Q3Q3 | Easy | Disordered substitutional

Explain the concept of disordered substitutional solid solution and state where it is normally observed.

Solution

In a disordered (random) substitutional solid solution, solute atoms replace solvent atoms on lattice sites with no pattern.

It is normally observed at high temperature, where thermal motion mixes the atoms. Example: Cu-Ni, atoms on random sites.

U3-Q4Q4 | Easy | Ordered substitutional

Explain the concept of ordered substitutional solid solution and describe how solute atoms are arranged in the lattice.

Solution

In an ordered substitutional solid solution the solute atoms take definite, regular positions in the lattice instead of random ones.

It is seen at lower temperature or at fixed atom ratios. Examples quoted in the notes: Monel and brass.

U3-Q5Q5 | Easy | Interstitial solid solution

Explain the formation mechanism of an interstitial solid solution with a suitable example.

Solution

Mechanism. The solute atoms do not replace solvent atoms. They enter the empty spaces (interstices) of the lattice. These spaces are small, so the solute atom must be small: radius below about 1 A. Transition elements such as Fe, Ti, V and Zr dissolve interstitial atoms easily.

Examples.

  • Carbon and nitrogen in ferrite, which harden and strengthen steel.
  • Hydrogen in steel during welding, which strains the lattice and causes hydrogen embrittlement.
U3-Q6Q6 | Easy | Hume-Rothery rules

Explain the four Hume-Rothery rules governing the formation of substitutional solid solutions.

Solution

Statement. Extensive substitutional solubility needs four conditions:

  1. Size factor: atomic radii differ by no more than about 15 %.
  2. Crystal structure: the same crystal structure (both FCC, both BCC or both HCP).
  3. Valence: the same valence.
  4. Electronegativity: nearly equal electronegativity.

Why each matters.

  • Size: a big gap strains the lattice.
  • Structure: with different structures solubility must stop where the structure changes.
  • Valence: a different valence favours compounds.
  • Electronegativity: a large difference makes electrons transfer and forms compounds (NaCl).

Example. Cu and Ni: similar size, both FCC, and they dissolve completely.

This is also Q&A answer 1 and assignment item 1. See Concepts B.

U3-Q7Q7 | Easy | Size factor

Explain the size-factor criterion of the Hume-Rothery rules and its importance in solid-solution formation.

Solution

Rule. The atomic radii of solute and solvent should differ by no more than about 15 % for extensive substitutional solubility (Q&A).

% size difference=rsolute−rsolventrsolvent×100\%\ \text{size difference}=\dfrac{r_{solute}-r_{solvent}}{r_{solvent}}\times100

Importance. A larger difference distorts the lattice, so the strain energy becomes too high and solubility is limited.

U3-Q8Q8 | Easy | Crystal structure and solubility

Explain the significance of crystal structure in determining solid solubility between two metals.

Solution

For complete solid solubility the two metals must have the same crystal structure.

If the structures differ, solubility must stop at some composition, where the structure changes. Cu and Ni are both FCC and dissolve completely.

U3-Q9Q9 | Easy | Valence and electronegativity

Explain the effect of valence and electronegativity on the formation of solid solutions.

Solution
  • Valence: the metals should have the same valence. A different valence favours compounds over solutions.
  • Electronegativity: the values should be nearly equal. A large difference makes electrons transfer, which forms compounds. Example from the notes: Na and Cl forming NaCl.

. Close values on both give solid solutions; large differences give compounds or limited solubility.

U3-Q10Q10 | Easy | Solubility limit

Define solubility limit and explain its significance in phase equilibrium.

Solution

Definition. The solubility limit is the largest solute concentration that still gives a single-phase solution at a given temperature.

Significance. Beyond the limit a second phase appears, so the phases present change.

Example. Sugar in water at 20 deg C: limit 64 wt% sugar. Below it, one phase (syrup). Above it, two phases: saturated syrup and solid sugar.

This is also Q&A answer 2. See Concepts C2.

Group Easy: Q11 to Q20

U3-Q11Q11 | Easy | Phase

Define a phase and explain the difference between a single-phase system and a multiphase mixture.

Solution

Definition. A phase is a homogeneous portion of a system with uniform physical and chemical properties.

  • Single-phase system (solution): only one phase, for example a syrup (sugar fully dissolved) or a solid solution.
  • Multiphase mixture: more than one phase, for example saturated syrup with solid sugar.

Example. Ice, liquid water and steam are three phases of the same substance.

U3-Q12Q12 | Easy | Equilibrium

Explain the concept of equilibrium in a material system in terms of temperature, pressure, composition, and free energy.

Solution

A system is at equilibrium when its free energy is at a minimum for the given temperature, pressure and composition.

If temperature, pressure or composition changes, the free energy rises. The system then moves to a new state of lower free energy. Equilibrium is the state that does not change with time at those conditions.

U3-Q13Q13 | Easy | Equilibrium phase diagram

Define an equilibrium phase diagram and explain the information that can be obtained from it.

Solution

Definition. An equilibrium (constitutional) phase diagram shows which phases exist at a given temperature and composition under slow cooling. It is a collection of solubility-limit curves.

Information:

  1. phases present at each composition and temperature
  2. solid solubility of one element in another
  3. temperature where solidification starts (liquidus) and ends (solidus)
  4. amount of each phase, by the lever rule
U3-Q14Q14 | Easy | No rate information

Explain why an equilibrium phase diagram does not provide information about the rate of phase transformation.

Solution

An equilibrium diagram describes the state reached after very slow cooling, when the system has had unlimited time. It gives which phases are stable, not how long they take to form.

Time does not appear on the diagram. With fast cooling, phases appear that the diagram does not show. That gap is filled by TTT diagrams (see Concepts K).

U3-Q15Q15 | Easy | Unary diagram of water

Explain the concept of a unary phase diagram using the pressure-temperature diagram of water.

Solution

Definition. A unary diagram is for a system of one component, here water. The variables are pressure and temperature.

Explanation. The plane has three regions: solid (ice), liquid and vapour. Lines between regions are where two phases coexist. The three lines meet at the triple point, where all three phases coexist.

Conclusion. Pressure and temperature fix the phase of the pure substance.

U3-Q16Q16 | Easy | Binary diagram

Explain the meaning of a binary phase diagram and identify its independent variables.

Solution

A binary phase diagram is for a system of two components, for example Cu-Ni.

The independent variables are temperature and composition. Pressure is fixed, almost always at 1 atm.

U3-Q17Q17 | Easy | Liquidus and solidus

Explain the terms liquidus and solidus lines with reference to a binary alloy phase diagram.

Solution
  • Liquidus: above this line the alloy is fully liquid. Solidification begins when cooling reaches it.
  • Solidus: at and below this line the alloy is fully solid. Above it, melting has begun.

Between the two lines the alloy is a mixture of liquid and solid. So an alloy freezes over a range of temperature, unlike a pure metal, which freezes at one temperature. Example melting points on the slide: Cu 1085 deg C, Ni 1453 deg C (C2).

See Concepts D3. This is also part of assignment item 2.

U3-Q18Q18 | Easy | Cu-Ni diagram

Explain the important features of the copper-nickel binary isomorphous phase diagram.

Solution

Isomorphous means complete solubility in the liquid and in the solid.

Features:

  1. Three regions: liquid (L) at high temperature, alpha + L (the two-phase zone between the lines), alpha (single-phase solid solution) at low temperature.
  2. Two lines: the liquidus and the solidus.
  3. Melting points of the pure metals: Cu 1085 deg C and Ni 1453 deg C.
  4. Cu and Ni are both FCC and similar in size, so they dissolve completely.

See Concepts D.

U3-Q19Q19 | Easy | Gibbs phase rule

State Gibbs phase rule and explain the meaning of each term in the phase-rule equation.

Solution

P+F=C+NP+F=C+N

  • P: number of phases present.
  • F: degrees of freedom, the variables (temperature, pressure, composition) that can change without changing the phases present.
  • C: number of components.
  • N: number of non-compositional variables (temperature and pressure).

At constant pressure N = 1, so F = C + 1 - P. Slide example: Cu-Ag, one phase, C = 2, N = 1, P = 1, so F = 2 + 1 - 1 = 2.

U3-Q20Q20 | Easy | Three rules for reading a diagram

Explain the three basic rules used to determine phases, phase compositions, and phase amounts from a binary phase diagram.

Solution

The slides give three rules:

  1. Phases present: if the temperature and the composition C0 are known, the diagram shows which phases are present.
  2. Phase compositions: the composition of each phase is read from the tie line.
  3. Phase amounts: the amount (fraction) of each phase is found with the lever rule.

Example: 1100 deg C and 60 wt% Ni lies in the alpha region, so only alpha is present. 1250 deg C and 35 wt% Ni lies in alpha + L, so both are present.

U3-Q22Q22 | Easy | Tie line

Explain how a tie line is constructed and used in a two-phase region of a binary phase diagram.

Solution

Construction:

  1. Draw a horizontal line (constant temperature) across the two-phase region at the alloy temperature.
  2. Mark the alloy composition C0 on it.
  3. The line ends where it meets the phase boundaries on the left and on the right.

Use. The two ends give the composition of each phase. With the lever rule the line also gives the amount of each phase.

U3-Q23Q23 | Easy | Lever rule principle

Explain the principle of the lever rule and its importance in determining phase fractions.

Solution

Principle. On the tie line, the fraction of one phase is the length of the arm from C0 to the boundary of the other phase, divided by the whole tie line. The amount of a phase is the lever arm on the opposite side.

WL=Cα−C0Cα−CLWα=C0−CLCα−CLW_L=\dfrac{C_\alpha-C_0}{C_\alpha-C_L}\qquad W_\alpha=\dfrac{C_0-C_L}{C_\alpha-C_L}

The fractions add to 1. Multiply by 100 for percent, or by the total mass for the mass of each phase.

Importance. It is the only way the diagram gives the amount of each phase.

Example: 35 wt% Ni at 1250 deg C: W_L = 8/11 = 0.727 and W_alpha = 3/11 = 0.273.

U3-Q24Q24 | Easy | Derive the lever rule

Derive the expressions used in the lever rule for determining the fractions of two phases.

Solution

The slides state the rule without a derivation; this uses mass balance (a step the slides skip).

Given: a two-phase alloy of overall composition C0 with phases L and alpha of compositions C_L and C_alpha.

Total mass balance:

WL+Wα=1W_L+W_\alpha=1

Balance of the solute (nickel):

C0=WLCL+WαCαC_0=W_LC_L+W_\alpha C_\alpha

Replace W_alpha by 1 - W_L:

C0=WLCL+(1−WL)CαC_0=W_LC_L+(1-W_L)C_\alpha

C0−Cα=WL(CL−Cα)C_0-C_\alpha=W_L(C_L-C_\alpha)

WL=Cα−C0Cα−CLW_L=\dfrac{C_\alpha-C_0}{C_\alpha-C_L}

Then:

Wα=1−WL=C0−CLCα−CLW_\alpha=1-W_L=\dfrac{C_0-C_L}{C_\alpha-C_L}

These are the lever-rule expressions.

U3-Q25Q25 | Easy | Compositions from a tie line

Explain how phase compositions are determined from the intersections of a tie line with phase boundaries.

Solution

At the alloy temperature draw the tie line across the two-phase region.

  • Where it meets the left boundary (the liquidus in Cu-Ni), read the composition of the liquid, C_L.
  • Where it meets the right boundary (the solidus), read the composition of the alpha, C_alpha.

Example: 1250 deg C, 35 wt% Ni gives C_L = 32 and C_alpha = 43 wt% Ni. reads 31.5 and 42.5 from the same tie line; this is a reading difference, not a different method.

In a single-phase region the phase has the alloy composition: at 1320 deg C only liquid with C_L = 35, at 1190 deg C only alpha with C_alpha = 35 wt% Ni.

U3-Q26Q26 | Easy | Solidification by nucleation and growth

Explain the process of solidification of metals through nucleation and growth.

Solution

Solidification happens in two stages:

  1. Nucleation: tiny stable solid particles (nuclei) form in the liquid.
  2. Growth: each nucleus grows by adding atoms until neighbouring crystals meet.

Some undercooling is needed first: at the melting point liquid and solid have the same free energy, so nothing freezes. The undercooling

ΔT=Tm−Tactual\Delta T=T_m-T_{actual}

gives the driving force. Many nuclei give many small crystals, few nuclei give a few large ones. The crystals become the grains of the solid.

U3-Q27Q27 | Easy | Nucleation and growth

Define nucleation and growth and explain their roles during metal solidification.

Solution
  • Nucleation: the formation of the first tiny stable solid particles (nuclei) in the liquid. It decides how many crystals there will be.
  • Growth: the increase in size of those nuclei as atoms join them. It decides how the crystals fill the space.

Together they set the number and size of grains.

U3-Q29Q29 | Easy | Embryo and nucleus

Explain the difference between an embryo and a stable nucleus during solidification.

Solution
  • Embryo: a tiny solid cluster smaller than the critical size. It is unstable: it may grow or dissolve again.
  • Nucleus: a cluster larger than the critical size. It is stable and keeps growing.

The dividing size is the critical radius r*.

U3-Q30Q30 | Easy | Critical nucleus and undercooling

Explain the concept of critical nucleus size and its relationship with undercooling.

Solution

The free-energy change for a solid sphere of radius r in the liquid is

ΔG=43πr3 ΔGv+4πr2 γsl\Delta G=\dfrac43\pi r^3\,\Delta G_v+4\pi r^2\,\gamma_{sl}

Setting the slope to zero gives the critical radius:

r∗=2γslTmΔHf ΔTr^*=\dfrac{2\gamma_{sl}T_m}{\Delta H_f\,\Delta T}

Clusters smaller than r* are embryos and larger ones are nuclei.

Relation to undercooling. DeltaT is in the denominator, so more undercooling gives a smaller r*. A smaller critical nucleus forms more easily.

U3-Q31Q31 | Easy | Heterogeneous nucleation needs less undercooling

Explain why heterogeneous nucleation requires less undercooling than homogeneous nucleation.

Solution

In homogeneous nucleation the nucleus forms inside the bulk liquid with no foreign surface. The solid-liquid surface energy is a large barrier, so a very high undercooling is needed.

In heterogeneous nucleation the nucleus forms on a foreign surface: an impurity particle, the mould wall or an added inoculant. The foreign surface lowers the barrier, so the cluster becomes stable at a very small undercooling, a fraction of a degree.

This is why inoculants are used for grain refinement.

U3-Q32Q32 | Easy | Dendrites

Explain the formation and characteristics of dendrites during metal solidification.

Solution

Formation. During alloy freezing, thermal and constitutional undercooling make parts of the solid-liquid surface push out into the liquid. Each protuberance grows as a branched, tree-like crystal: a dendrite.

Characteristics.

  • a main branch with many side branches, like a pine tree
  • they grow along fixed lattice directions
  • latent heat of fusion released at the growing solid warms the liquid ahead back to the freezing temperature

U3-Q35Q35 | Easy | Grain refinement

Explain the concept of grain refinement and its significance in engineering materials.

Solution

Definition. Grain refinement is the addition of heterogeneous nuclei, in a controlled way, to increase the number of grains in a casting. More grains mean smaller grains.

How. Add inoculants (grain refiners). They give nucleation sites at very small undercooling.

Significance. It is one of the applications of controlled nucleation, with dispersion strengthening, solid-state transformation and rapid solidification processing. The course files give no numbers on how grain size changes properties, so none are quoted.

U3-Q38Q38 | Easy | Why 6.67 wt% C

Explain why the iron-carbon diagram generally considers the Fe-Fe3C system up to 6.67 wt% carbon.

Solution

The right-hand axis is not 100 % carbon. It stops at 6.67 wt% C, where the compound iron carbide (Fe3C, cementite) forms. Carbon beyond this limit would not dissolve in iron.

All real steels and cast irons have less than 6.67 wt% C, so only the Fe-Fe3C part of the diagram is used.

U3-Q39Q39 | Easy | Ferrite

Explain the crystal structure, carbon solubility, and characteristics of ferrite.

Solution
  • Structure: BCC (alpha iron).
  • Carbon solubility: very small, a maximum of 0.022 wt% at 727 deg C and 0.008 wt% at room temperature. It is an interstitial solid solution; BCC has small voids.
  • Characteristics: the softest phase, ductile and magnetic.
U3-Q40Q40 | Easy | Austenite

Explain the crystal structure, carbon solubility, and characteristics of austenite.

Solution
  • Structure: FCC (gamma iron).
  • Carbon solubility: up to 2.14 wt% at 1147 deg C. FCC has larger octahedral voids than BCC, so it holds more carbon than ferrite.
  • Characteristics: soft and formable, non-magnetic. Most heat treatments start in this phase. It is not stable below 727 deg C.

U3-Q41Q41 | Easy | Cementite

Explain the structure and properties of cementite and discuss why it is considered a hard and brittle phase.

Solution
  • Structure: cementite is iron carbide, Fe3C, with an orthorhombic structure.

U3-Q42Q42 | Easy | Pearlite

Explain the formation, composition, and structure of pearlite in the iron-carbon system.

Solution

Formation. Pearlite forms at 727 deg C by the eutectoid decomposition of austenite. The austenite wants to become BCC ferrite, which holds only about 0.022 wt% C. The extra carbon forms cementite, and carbon diffuses between the two.

γ (0.76) ⟶ α (0.022)+Fe3C (6.67)\gamma\ (0.76)\ \longrightarrow\ \alpha\ (0.022)+\mathrm{Fe_3C}\ (6.67)

Composition. 0.76 wt% C overall.

Structure. A fine mixture of ferrite and cementite in alternate plates (lamellae). Under a microscope it looks like mother-of-pearl, hence the name. It grows from the grain boundary into the unreacted austenite grain.

Also part of assignment item 3. See Concepts J3 to J4.

U3-Q47Q47 | Easy | Eutectoid transformation

Explain the transformation of austenite into ferrite and cementite during eutectoid transformation.

Solution

At 727 deg C iron wants to change from FCC austenite to BCC ferrite. BCC ferrite can hold only about 0.022 wt% C, so the extra carbon is pushed out and forms cementite, Fe3C, with 6.67 wt% C.

Carbon diffuses away from the 0.022 wt% ferrite regions to the 6.67 wt% cementite layers. The product is a layered mixture, pearlite.

U3-Q48Q48 | Easy | Hypo-eutectoid steel on slow cooling

Explain the microstructural development of hypo-eutectoid steel during slow cooling.

Solution

For carbon below 0.76 wt% (the slide uses point C at 875 deg C and D at 775 deg C):

  1. At point C: all austenite.
  2. On cooling to point D the steel enters the ferrite + austenite region. Low-carbon ferrite forms first and grows along the austenite grain boundaries.
  3. The remaining austenite is richer in carbon. Cooling to just above 727 deg C gives more ferrite and richer austenite.
  4. At 727 deg C the remaining austenite has exactly 0.76 wt% C and changes to pearlite.

Final structure: pro-eutectoid ferrite (formed before the eutectoid reaction) plus pearlite: bright ferrite grains in dark pearlite colonies.

Austenite holds up to 0.76 wt% C at this point, ferrite only about 0.022 wt% and cementite 6.67 wt%. To form them, carbon must move: away from the growing ferrite regions and into the cementite layers.

Temperature controls the result (topic K): just below 727 deg C diffusion is fast, so the layers are thick (coarse pearlite). At lower temperature diffusion is slow, so the layers are thin (fine pearlite).

Solution

U3-Q62Q62 | Medium | Atomic size mismatch and lattice strain

Explain how atomic-size mismatch can produce lattice strain and influence the solid solubility of an alloying element.

Solution

Principle. A substitutional atom sits on a lattice site of the solvent. If its size differs from the solvent atom, the neighbouring atoms are pushed apart or squeezed together around it. The lattice is distorted (strained) around each solute atom (Q&A 1).

Effect on solubility.

  • Small mismatch (within about 15 %): little distortion, so the strain energy is low and the alloy can hold a lot of solute, even complete solubility (Cu-Ni).
  • Large mismatch: severe lattice distortion, so the strain energy becomes too high, and solid solubility is reduced. A second phase or a compound forms instead (for example Cu-Zn beyond 30 % Zn, where zinc has limited solubility in copper).

Measure. The percentage size difference formula, with the 15 % limit.

Conclusion. Size mismatch controls how much solute the lattice can accept, and it is the first of the four rules. The other three rules must also be met.

U3-Q68Q68 | Medium | Undercooling and critical nucleus size

Explain how increasing undercooling influences critical nucleus size and the nucleation process during solidification.

Solution

Principle. The free-energy change of a solid sphere of radius r in the liquid is

ΔG=43πr3 ΔGv+4πr2 γsl\Delta G=\dfrac43\pi r^3\,\Delta G_v+4\pi r^2\,\gamma_{sl}

The first term (volume, negative on freezing) favours the solid. The second (surface) opposes it. Setting the slope to zero gives the critical radius

r∗=2γslTmΔHf ΔTr^*=\dfrac{2\gamma_{sl}T_m}{\Delta H_f\,\Delta T}

Effect of undercooling. The undercooling Delta T = T_m - T_actual is in the denominator. More undercooling gives a smaller r*.

  • A smaller critical nucleus forms more easily, so many more clusters exceed r* and become stable nuclei.
  • More nuclei give many small crystals, so a fine grain structure.
  • At small undercooling r* is large, few nuclei form, and the grains are large.

Clusters smaller than r* are embryos and may dissolve again. Larger ones are nuclei and grow.

Conclusion. Higher undercooling means a smaller critical nucleus and an easier, faster start of solidification.

U3-Q69Q69 | Medium | Heterogeneous nucleation for grain refinement

U3-Q70Q70 | Medium | Coarse columnar grains

A casting develops coarse columnar grains; explain the solidification conditions responsible for this structure and suggest how grain refinement could be promoted.

Solution

Conditions that give columnar grains.

  • Heat is removed through the cold mould wall, so freezing moves inward from the wall.
  • Slow cooling with a steep temperature gradient.
  • Few nuclei ahead of the front. Only grains whose easy growth direction points across the mould (perpendicular to the wall) keep growing, and they grow inward as long, thin, coarse columns.

Promoting grain refinement.

  1. Add inoculants (grain refiners) so heterogeneous nucleation gives many nuclei at small undercooling.
  2. Increase the number of nucleation points in general: the final grain size depends on the number of nucleation points.
  3. Aim for the equiaxed condition: many nuclei, grains growing about equally in all directions.

Conclusion. Columnar grains come from few nuclei and directional heat flow. More nuclei, from inoculants, give finer equiaxed grains.

U3-Q89Q89 | Medium | Designing a hardening treatment

A steel component requires increased hardness through the formation of martensite; explain how the hardening treatment should be designed.

Solution

Principle. Hardening heats the steel to an austenitic structure, holds it, and quenches it fast enough to form martensite. Martensite is the hard phase (BCT).

Design steps.

  1. Heating temperature. Hypo-eutectoid steel: 30 to 50 deg C above A3, so the structure is completely austenite. Hyper-eutectoid steel: 30 to 50 deg C above A1, so only the pearlite becomes austenite and cementite is retained before quenching.
  2. Soaking. Hold long enough to be fully austenitic.
  3. Quench. Quench in water, oil or brine faster than the critical cooling rate, so the TTT nose is missed (topic L, curves 6 and 7).

  6. After the quench. As-quenched martensite is brittle, so temper it to relieve stress and restore toughness.

Conclusion. Austenitize correctly, quench faster than CCR, then temper.

Selection.

  1. Low-carbon steel cannot be hardened by heating and quenching alone because it has too little carbon.
  2. Induction and flame hardening only change the structure of the surface by fast heating and quenching. They need adequate carbon and alloying (hardenability) to give martensite. Low-carbon steel does not meet this.
  3. Carburizing adds carbon to the skin first, so the skin can then harden on quenching. The core keeps its low carbon content and stays ductile.

Conclusion. Low carbon in the core is the requirement, and carbon addition is what makes the skin hardenable, so carburizing is selected. This is also part of assignment item 5. See Q93, Q94.
