A. Solid solutions

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definition

An alloy is a solid solution of two or more metals, mixed uniformly in the solid state.

  • Solvent: the base metal, the host lattice.
  • Solute: the alloying element, dissolved in the host.

2. Types of solid solution

A. Substitutional: the solute atom takes the place of a solvent atom on a normal lattice site.

  • Disordered (random): no pattern, common at high temperature.
  • Ordered: definite regular positions, seen at lower temperature or fixed atom ratios.
  • Examples: Cu in Ni (Monel), brass.

B. Interstitial: the solute atom sits in the gaps between solvent atoms.

  • The solute must be small: atomic radius below about 1 A.
  • Examples: carbon and nitrogen in ferrite harden steel; hydrogen in steel during welding causes embrittlement.

Exam-ready conclusion: a solid solution has one phase with uniformly distributed atoms; substitutional solutes replace solvent atoms, interstitial solutes occupy the gaps.

A1. Why start here

Structure decides properties.

Change the structure by changing the composition and the heat treatment, and the properties change.

Example from the slides: a knee-joint replacement.

The metal parts must resist corrosion and fatigue, so the alloy and its structure are chosen with care.

A2. What is a solid solution

An alloy is a solid solution of two or more metals, mixed uniformly in the solid state.

Two words to know:

  • Solvent: the base metal, the host lattice.
  • Solute: the alloying element, dissolved in the host.

Everyday example: sugar stirred into tea.

Tea is the solvent, sugar is the solute, and you see only one liquid. A solid solution is the same idea with atoms in a crystal.

A3. Two types

Solid solutions are grouped by where the solute atoms sit.

  1. Substitutional: the solute atom takes the place of a solvent atom on a normal lattice site.
  2. Interstitial: the solute atom sits in the gaps (interstices) between solvent atoms.

A4. Substitutional: disordered and ordered

Substitutional solid solutions have two sub-types.

  • Disordered (random): solute atoms replace solvent atoms with no pattern. Common at high temperature, where thermal motion mixes the atoms.
  • Ordered: solute atoms take definite, regular positions. Seen at lower temperature or at fixed atom ratios.

Examples quoted in the notes: Monel (copper dissolved in nickel) and brass.

A5. Interstitial

Here the solute atoms do not replace anything.

They enter the empty spaces of the lattice.

Those spaces are small, so the solute atom must be small.

The slides give the rule: atomic radius below about 1 A.

Transition elements (Fe, Ti, V, Zr) dissolve interstitially very easily.

Examples from the notes:

  • Carbon and nitrogen in ferrite. They harden and strengthen steel.
  • Hydrogen in steel during welding. It strains the lattice and causes hydrogen embrittlement.

A7. Limited and complete solubility (picture)

The slides show three cases (Callister figure):

  • Liquid copper and liquid nickel mix completely.
  • Solid Cu-Ni shows complete solid solubility, atoms on random sites.
  • In Cu-Zn alloys with more than 30 % Zn, a second phase appears, because zinc has limited solubility in copper.

B. Hume-Rothery rules

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definition

The Hume-Rothery rules are four rules that decide when one metal can dissolve substitutionally in another. If all four are met, solubility can be large, even complete.

2. The four rules

Rule Condition Reason
Size factorradii differ by no more than about 15 %a big gap strains the lattice
Crystal structuresame structure (both FCC, BCC or HCP)with different structures solubility must stop somewhere
Valencesame valencea different valence favours compounds
Electronegativitynearly equala large difference favours compounds, for example NaCl

3. Size-factor formula

% size difference=rsolute−rsolventrsolvent×100\%\ \text{size difference}=\dfrac{r_{solute}-r_{solvent}}{r_{solvent}}\times100

The rule asks for a value within 15 %.

4. Example

Cu and Ni have similar atomic size and both are FCC, so they dissolve completely in the liquid and the solid.

Exam-ready conclusion: maximum substitutional solubility is favoured by similar atomic size, the same crystal structure, the same valence and similar electronegativity.

B1. What the rules decide

The four rules say when one metal can dissolve substitutionally in another.

If all four are met, solubility can be large, even complete.

If one is badly broken, solubility is limited or a compound forms.

B2. The four rules

  1. Size factor: the atomic radii must differ by no more than about 15 %.
  2. Crystal structure: the two metals must have the same crystal structure (both FCC, both BCC or both HCP).
  3. Valence: the two must have the same valence.
  4. Electronegativity: the electronegativity values must be nearly equal.

B3. Why each rule matters, in one line each

  • Size: a big size gap strains the lattice, and the strain energy becomes too high.
  • Crystal structure: with different structures the solubility must stop at some point, where the structure changes.
  • Valence: different valence favours compounds over solutions.
  • Electronegativity: a large difference makes electrons transfer, which forms compounds. Na and Cl forming NaCl is the notes' example.

B4. The size-factor number

The percentage size difference is

% size difference=rsolute−rsolventrsolvent×100\%\ \text{size difference}=\dfrac{r_{solute}-r_{solvent}}{r_{solvent}}\times100

and the rule asks for a value within 15 %.

(This formula is written out in the Q&A model answer; the notes and slides state the 15 % limit.)

B5. Example: Cu and Ni

Copper and nickel have similar atomic sizes (Q&A answer 1) and the same crystal structure, FCC.

They dissolve in each other completely, in the liquid and in the solid state.

This is why Cu-Ni is the standard isomorphous example (topic D).

B6. How to use the rules

For two candidate metals, check the rules in this order, and stop at the first clear failure:

  1. Size: compute the percentage difference.
  2. Crystal structure: same or different.
  3. Valence: same or different.
  4. Electronegativity: close or far apart.

All four satisfied: expect extensive substitutional solid solution.

C. Phase, equilibrium and phase diagrams

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definitions

  • Phase: a homogeneous portion of a system with uniform physical and chemical properties. Ice, water and steam are three phases.
  • Solution: a single phase. Mixture: more than one phase.
  • Solubility limit: the largest solute concentration that still gives a single-phase solution at a given temperature. Beyond it a second phase appears.
  • Equilibrium: the free energy of the system is at a minimum for the given temperature, pressure and composition.
  • Equilibrium phase diagram: shows which phases exist for given temperature and composition under slow cooling; also called a constitutional diagram.

2. Example

Sugar in water at 20 deg C: solubility limit 64 wt% sugar. Below it, one phase (syrup). Above it, two phases (saturated syrup and solid sugar).

3. What a phase diagram gives

  1. Phases present at each temperature and composition.
  2. Solid solubility of one element in another.
  3. Start and end of solidification (liquidus and solidus).
  4. Amount of each phase by the lever rule.

Limitation: it gives equilibrium only, not the rate of transformation. TTT diagrams cover the rate.

4. Unary and binary diagrams

Unary Binary
Componentsone (water)two (Cu-Ni)
Variablestemperature and pressuretemperature and composition (pressure fixed at 1 atm)

Exam-ready conclusion: a phase diagram is a map of the stable phases for temperature and composition at equilibrium; it gives phases, solubility limits, solidification range and phase amounts, but not transformation rates.

C1. Phase, solution, mixture

  • Phase: a homogeneous portion of a system with uniform physical and chemical properties. Ice, liquid water and steam are three phases of the same substance.
  • Solution: a single phase (solid, liquid or gas).
  • Mixture: more than one phase.

C2. Solubility limit

The solubility limit is the largest solute concentration that still gives a single-phase solution at a given temperature.

Go beyond it and a second phase appears.

Examples from the slides:

  • Water and alcohol: unlimited solubility.
  • Salt and water: limited solubility.
  • Oil and water: almost none.

Worked example from the slides, sugar and water at 20 deg C:

the solubility limit is 64 wt% sugar.

Below 64 wt% sugar you have one phase, a syrup (liquid solution).

Above 64 wt% you have two phases: the saturated syrup and solid sugar.

C3. Equilibrium

A system is at equilibrium when its free energy is at a minimum for the given temperature, pressure and composition.

Change T, P or composition, and the free energy rises.

The system then moves to a new state with lower free energy.

C4. Equilibrium phase diagram

An equilibrium (or constitutional) phase diagram shows which phases exist, for given temperature and composition, under slow cooling.

It is a collection of solubility-limit curves.

What it tells you:

  1. The phases present at each composition and temperature.
  2. The solid solubility of one element in another.
  3. The temperature where solidification starts (liquidus) and where it ends (solidus).
  4. The amount of each phase, by the lever rule (topic E).

What it does not tell you: how fast a transformation happens.

It gives equilibrium only, not the rate.

That gap is filled by the TTT diagrams in topic K.

C5. Unary diagram (one component)

A system with one component, such as water, has a unary phase diagram.

The variables are temperature and pressure.

C6. Binary diagram (two components)

A binary diagram has two components.

The variables are temperature and composition.

Pressure is fixed at 1 atm, almost always.

D. Cu-Ni isomorphous diagram and the Gibbs phase rule

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definitions

  • Isomorphous system: the two components dissolve in each other completely, in the liquid and in the solid. Cu-Ni is the classic example.
  • Liquidus: the line below which solidification begins; above it the alloy is fully liquid.
  • Solidus: the line at and below which the alloy is completely solid.

2. Regions of the Cu-Ni diagram

Region Phases Where
Lliquidhigh temperature
alpha + Lliquid and solidbetween liquidus and solidus
alphasolid solutionlow temperature

A pure metal freezes at one temperature (Cu 1085 deg C, Ni 1453 deg C on the slide); an alloy freezes over a range.

3. Reading the diagram

  1. Temperature and composition C0 give the phases present.
  2. They also give the composition of each phase (tie line).
  3. They also give the amount of each phase (lever rule).

4. Gibbs phase rule

P+F=C+NP+F=C+N

P phases present, F degrees of freedom, C components, N non-compositional variables (temperature and pressure). At constant pressure N = 1:

P+F=C+1P+F=C+1

Example: Cu-Ag single phase, C = 2, P = 1, so F = 2 + 1 - 1 = 2.

Exam-ready conclusion: the Cu-Ni diagram has liquid, alpha + L and alpha regions; the phase rule at constant pressure is P + F = C + 1.

D1. Isomorphous means complete solubility

In a binary isomorphous system the two components dissolve in each other completely, in the liquid and in the solid.

Copper-nickel (Cu-Ni) is the classic example.

D2. Three regions

The Cu-Ni diagram has three regions:

  1. Liquid (L): high temperature.
  2. alpha + L: the two-phase "mushy" zone between the liquidus and the solidus.
  3. alpha: a single-phase solid solution at low temperature.

D3. Liquidus and solidus

  • Liquidus: below this line solidification begins. Above it the alloy is fully liquid.
  • Solidus: at and below this line the alloy is completely solid. Above it, melting has begun.

So the freezing of an alloy covers a range of temperature, from the liquidus down to the solidus.

(A pure metal freezes at one temperature. The melting points on the slide: Cu 1085 deg C, Ni 1453 deg C.)

D4. Reading the diagram: three rules

The slides give three rules ( to).

Rule 1 says: if you know the temperature and the composition C0, you know which phases are present.

Rule 2 says: you also know the composition of each phase.

Rule 3 says: you also know the amount of each phase.

Rule 1 is explained here. Rules 2 and 3 are in topic E.

D5. Rule 1 worked on the slide

Slide example: two points of the Cu-Ni diagram.

  • Point A: 1100 deg C, 60 wt% Ni. It lies in the alpha region, so only alpha is present.
  • Point B: 1250 deg C, 35 wt% Ni. It lies in the alpha + L region, so both alpha and L are present.

(The slide marks which region each point is in; this note repeats those readings.)

D6. Effect of temperature and composition

A change of temperature at fixed composition can move the alloy from one region to another (path A to B on the slide).

A change of composition at fixed temperature can do the same (path B to C).

D7. Gibbs phase rule

The rule is

P+F=C+NP+F=C+N

and the terms mean:

  • P: number of phases present.
  • F: degrees of freedom, the variables (temperature, pressure, composition) you can change without changing the phases present.
  • C: number of components.
  • N: number of non-compositional variables, here temperature and pressure.

At constant pressure (1 atm), N = 1 and the rule becomes

P+F=C+1P+F=C+1

D8. Slide example and two more

Slide example: a Cu-Ag alloy, single phase, N = 1 (temperature only), C = 2, P = 1.

F=C+N−PF=C+N-P

F=2+1−1=2F=2+1-1=2

The same rule applied to the two-phase region of a binary diagram (C = 2, N = 1, P = 2):

F=2+1−2=1F=2+1-2=1

Meaning: at a chosen temperature inside the two-phase region the phase compositions are fixed. This is why a tie line exists at each temperature.

(The two-phase case applies the rule the slide gives; the slide itself works only the single-phase case.)

E. Tie line and lever rule

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Tie line

In a two-phase region:

  1. Draw a horizontal line at the alloy temperature across the region.
  2. Read the composition where it meets the left boundary and the right boundary.
  3. The two ends give the phase compositions.

Slide example: 35 wt% Ni at 1250 deg C gives CL=32C_L=32 and Cα=43C_\alpha=43 wt% Ni.

2. Lever rule

The fraction of a phase is the lever arm on the opposite side divided by the whole tie line.

WL=Cα−C0Cα−CLWα=C0−CLCα−CLWL+Wα=1W_L=\dfrac{C_\alpha-C_0}{C_\alpha-C_L}\qquad W_\alpha=\dfrac{C_0-C_L}{C_\alpha-C_L}\qquad W_L+W_\alpha=1

Mass of a phase = fraction x total mass.

3. Derivation (mass balance; a step the slides skip)

C0=WLCL+WαCα,Wα=1−WL ⇒ WL=Cα−C0Cα−CLC_0=W_LC_L+W_\alpha C_\alpha,\quad W_\alpha=1-W_L\ \Rightarrow\ W_L=\dfrac{C_\alpha-C_0}{C_\alpha-C_L}

4. Worked example

WL=43−3543−32=0.727Wα=35−3243−32=0.273W_L=\dfrac{43-35}{43-32}=0.727\qquad W_\alpha=\dfrac{35-32}{43-32}=0.273

So 72.7 % liquid and 27.3 % alpha. reads the same tie line as 31.5 and 42.5, giving 68 % and 32 %; state which reading you use.

Exam-ready conclusion: the tie line gives the compositions of the two phases and the lever rule gives their amounts.

E1. Tie line

In a two-phase region, the compositions of the two phases are read with a tie line.

  1. Draw a horizontal line (constant temperature) across the two-phase region at the alloy's temperature.
  2. Where it meets the phase boundary on the left, read that composition.
  3. Where it meets the boundary on the right, read that composition.

The two ends give the composition of each phase.

E2. Slide example: 35 wt% Ni at 1250 deg C

At 1250 deg C (point C on the slide) both alpha and liquid are present.

The tie line gives:

CL=32 wt% NiC_L=32\ \text{wt\% Ni}

Cα=43 wt% NiC_\alpha=43\ \text{wt\% Ni}

At 1320 deg C (point A) only liquid is present, so C_L = C0 = 35 wt% Ni.

At 1190 deg C (point B) only alpha is present, so C_alpha = C0 = 35 wt% Ni.

E3. Lever rule: the procedure

The slides give the steps:

  1. Construct the tie line across the two-phase region at the alloy's temperature.
  2. Locate the overall alloy composition C0 on the tie line.
  3. The fraction of one phase is the length of the tie line from C0 to the boundary of the other phase, divided by the length of the whole tie line.
  4. Find the other phase the same way.
  5. For percentages, multiply by 100.

On a weight-percent axis the fractions are mass fractions.

Mass of a phase = phase fraction multiplied by total alloy mass.

Memory aid: the amount of a phase is the lever arm on the opposite side.

E4. The equations

For a liquid L and a solid alpha:

WL=Cα−C0Cα−CLW_L=\dfrac{C_\alpha-C_0}{C_\alpha-C_L}

Wα=C0−CLCα−CLW_\alpha=\dfrac{C_0-C_L}{C_\alpha-C_L}

and the two fractions add to 1:

WL+Wα=1W_L+W_\alpha=1

E5. Why these equations are true (short derivation)

The slides state the rule without a derivation, so this step is filled in from mass balance (a step the slides skip).

Total mass balance:

WL+Wα=1W_L+W_\alpha=1

Balance of the component (here nickel):

C0=WLCL+WαCαC_0=W_LC_L+W_\alpha C_\alpha

Replace W_alpha by 1 minus W_L:

C0=WLCL+(1−WL)CαC_0=W_LC_L+(1-W_L)C_\alpha

WL=Cα−C0Cα−CLW_L=\dfrac{C_\alpha-C_0}{C_\alpha-C_L}

and then W_alpha follows from W_L + W_alpha = 1.

E6. Worked example

Given: C0 = 35 wt% Ni, 1250 deg C, C_L = 32, C_alpha = 43.

WL=43−3543−32=811=0.727W_L=\dfrac{43-35}{43-32}=\dfrac{8}{11}=0.727

Wα=35−3243−32=311=0.273W_\alpha=\dfrac{35-32}{43-32}=\dfrac{3}{11}=0.273

So 73 wt% liquid and 27 wt% alpha (the slide's result, recomputed).

Check: 0.727 + 0.273 = 1.

repeats the example with C_alpha = 42.5 and C_L = 31.5 wt% Ni (the Callister reading at 1250 deg C):

WL=42.5−3542.5−31.5=7.511=0.68W_L=\dfrac{42.5-35}{42.5-31.5}=\dfrac{7.5}{11}=0.68

Wα=35−31.542.5−31.5=3.511=0.32W_\alpha=\dfrac{35-31.5}{42.5-31.5}=\dfrac{3.5}{11}=0.32

E8. Mass of each phase

If the total alloy mass is M, then

ML=WL MM_L=W_L\,M

Mα=Wα MM_\alpha=W_\alpha\,M

Example: M = 10 kg of the 35 wt% Ni alloy at 1250 deg C (readings 32 and 43):

F. Solidification: nucleation and growth

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Definition

Solidification is the change from liquid to solid by nucleation and growth.

  1. Nucleation: tiny stable solid nuclei form in the liquid.

Nothing freezes at exactly that temperature.

F2. Two stages

Solidification goes by nucleation and growth.

  1. Nucleation: tiny stable solid particles (nuclei) form in the liquid.
  2. Growth: each nucleus grows by adding atoms, until neighbouring crystals meet.

Many nuclei give many small crystals.

Few nuclei give a few large crystals.

F3. Embryo and nucleus

  • Embryo: a tiny solid cluster that is too small, so it is unstable. It may grow or dissolve again.
  • Nucleus: a cluster larger than the critical size, so it is stable and grows.

G1. From crystals to grains

Many nuclei form in the liquid, and each grows into a crystal.

A solidified metal with many crystals is polycrystalline.

The crystals are called grains.

The surfaces between grains are grain boundaries.

The final grain size depends on the number of nucleation points.

G2. Dendrites

During alloy freezing, thermal and constitutional undercooling make parts of the solid-liquid surface push out into the liquid.

Each protuberance grows as a branched, tree-like crystal called a dendrite.

A dendrite has a main branch with many side branches. It looks like a pine tree.

Dendrites form because crystals grow along fixed lattice directions.

Latent heat of fusion is released as the solid grows. It warms the liquid ahead back to the freezing temperature.

G3. Three zones of a cast ingot

Liquid metal poured into a mould freezes from the cold wall inward.

This gives three zones, from the wall to the centre:

  1. Chill zone: next to the wall, a thin layer of small, randomly oriented equiaxed grains.
  2. Columnar zone: long, parallel grains growing inward, at right angles to the mould wall.
  3. Equiaxed zone: in the centre, randomly oriented equiaxed grains.

G4. Why each zone forms

  • Chill zone: the wall is cold, so undercooling is very large and the nucleation rate is high. Many nuclei, so small grains.
  • Columnar zone: the grains of the chill zone compete. Only grains with an easy growth direction pointing across the mould (perpendicular to the wall) keep growing, and they grow inward as long columns.
  • Equiaxed zone: the centre contains free-floating nuclei or broken dendrite pieces. They freeze in random orientations.

J3. What happens at 727 deg C

Iron wants to change from FCC austenite to BCC ferrite.

BCC ferrite can hold only about 0.02 wt% C (0.022).

The extra carbon is pushed out and forms cementite, Fe3C, with 6.7 wt% C.

This needs carbon to diffuse.

Carbon moves away from the 0.022 wt% ferrite regions to the 6.7 wt% cementite layers.

That is why the product is layered: with thin layers, carbon only has to diffuse a very short distance.

The pearlite grows from the grain boundary into the unreacted austenite grain.

The Fe-Fe3C diagram is for very slow cooling only.

With faster cooling, other phases appear that are not on that diagram. That is its major drawback.

The TTT (time-temperature-transformation) diagram shows these non-equilibrium products.

One diagram is for one composition only.

Other names: isothermal transformation diagram, Bain's curve, C-curve.

K2. How the diagram is made

give the method:

  1. Cut many identical small samples from one bar. Small pieces react quickly to a temperature change.
  2. Heat all of them in a furnace until they are fully austenite.
  3. Move a set of samples into a molten salt bath held at one constant temperature below 727 deg C (the slide example is 675 deg C).
  4. Hold each sample for a different time, then quench it.
  5. Check hardness and look at the microstructure.
  6. Repeat at other temperatures until there are enough points.

Each temperature gives an S-shaped curve of percent transformed against time (: begins, 50 %, ends).

Joining the "begins" points and the "ends" points of all temperatures gives two parallel C-shaped curves.

Temperature is on the vertical axis. Time, on a log scale, is on the horizontal axis.

  • Left of the start curve: austenite, but unstable below 727 deg C (it has not had time to change).
  • Between the two curves: austenite changing into product.
  • Right of the finish curve: transformation complete.
  • Two horizontal lines, Ms and Mf: start and finish of martensite.

The diagram is for isothermal transformation: the steel is cooled quickly to a temperature and then held there.

  • It is metastable: it stays at room temperature but changes to equilibrium phases if heated.
  • It is the hardest phase of iron and carbon, and its hardness rises with carbon content.
  • It is very brittle, because of high internal strain.

M1. What heat treatment is

Heat treatment is a combination of heating and cooling operations on a solid metal or alloy, to produce a particular microstructure and so the wanted properties.

If the composition is fixed and the part has already solidified, only heat treatment can change its properties.

M2. Objectives

From:

  • improve strength, impact resistance, ductility
  • relieve internal stresses left by casting, welding, forging or machining
  • refine grains
  • soften the metal for machinability and formability
  • improve wear, abrasion and corrosion resistance
  • change magnetic and electrical properties
  • make a hard surface with a softer interior
  • remove the effect of strain hardening and restore ductility of cold-worked metal

M3. Three stages

  1. Heat to the prescribed temperature.
  2. Hold (soak) there for a known time.
  3. Cool at the rate needed for the wanted microstructure.

Processes are grouped as full heat treatment and surface heat treatment.

M4. Critical temperatures

  • A1 (lower critical): below it no austenite is present.
  • A3 (upper critical): above it the steel is all austenite.
  • Acm: the cementite line for hyper-eutectoid steel.
  • Between A1 and A3 (or Acm) the steel is ferrite + austenite (or cementite + austenite).
  • Different carbon contents have different A3 and Acm but the same A1.

M5. Annealing

Annealing heats a metal and cools it slowly so that its structure settles into an ordered arrangement. It is used most for iron and steel.

Stages: heat, soak, cool, usually to room temperature.

Purposes: relieve stresses, make the metal soft, change ductility and toughness, refine grain, produce a definite microstructure.

It is used on castings, forgings, rolled stock and press work.

Types: full, process, stress-relief, recrystallization and spheroidise annealing.

Exam-ready answer: copy and write

1. Why special quenches

In a thick section the surface cools faster than the core, so the core may form bainite or pearlite instead of martensite.

2. Martempering

  1. Quench austenitized steel in a salt bath just above Ms (slide: 20 to 30 deg C above Ms, about 180 to 250 deg C).
  2. Hold briefly so the temperature evens out.
  3. Cool in air; the whole section turns to martensite together.

Result: martensite with minimum distortion and small thermal and structural stress.

3. Austempering

  1. Quench in a salt bath above Ms (slide: 300 to 400 deg C).
  2. Hold until isothermal transformation to lower bainite is complete.
  3. Cool at any rate; no tempering needed.

Advantages: better ductility at the same hardness, no distortion or cracks, better impact strength, high endurance limit.

O1. What it is

Precipitation hardening (age hardening) raises strength and hardness by forming very fine precipitate particles inside a softer matrix.

It is used on non-ferrous alloys such as Al-Cu, Al-Mg-Si, Al-Zn-Mg, Cu-Be and some nickel-based alloys.

O2. Three stages

The slides use an Al-Cu alloy.

  1. Solution treatment. Heat to a temperature where the solute (Cu) dissolves completely in the matrix (Al), giving a single-phase solid solution.
  2. Quenching. Cool rapidly to room temperature. The solute atoms have no time to form equilibrium precipitates, so they are trapped. The result is a supersaturated solid solution (SSSS).
  3. Aging. Reheat to a moderate temperature (artificial aging) or hold at room temperature (natural aging). Solute atoms gather into clusters and then fine precipitates.

O3. Why it hardens

The precipitates are fine and spread through the metal. They obstruct the movement of dislocations. This raises yield strength and hardness.

Aging is the most important stage: the unstable supersaturated solution releases its excess atoms as small particles called precipitates.

lists the order of events during aging: supersaturated solid solution, solute clustering, nucleation, growth, coarsening.

Everyday picture: sand stirred into thick syrup makes the syrup harder to move. The precipitates are the sand grains, and a dislocation is the thing trying to move.

1. Definition and purpose

Surface heat treatment hardens only the outer layer, leaving a tough core, for gears, cams, crankshafts and dies that need wear resistance and fatigue life.

  • Case hardening: surface composition changed (carburizing, nitriding).

P1. Why only the surface

Gears, pistons, crankshafts, cams and dies need a hard outer surface for wear resistance and fatigue life, with a tough core.

So only the surface is hardened. There are two groups:

  • Case hardening: the surface composition is changed (carburizing, nitriding).

Low-carbon steel cannot be hardened by heating alone because it has too little carbon. Case hardening gives a hard skin over a softer core.
